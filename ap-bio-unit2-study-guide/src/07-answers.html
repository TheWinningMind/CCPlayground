Answers: Check Your Understanding

Topic 2.1

1. (C) Ribosomes are found in all cells. Only eukaryotes have a nucleus (A), mitochondria (B), and a Golgi complex (D).
2. (D) A cell wall, ribosomes, and a single circular chromosome with no membrane-bound organelles describe a prokaryote such as a bacterium. Plant, animal, and fungal cells all have a nucleus.
3. (A) Shared, similar ribosomes and a shared genetic code are best explained by inheritance from a common ancestor. Ribosomes have no membrane (C), and independent origin (D) would not produce such similarity.
4. (B) Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes for intracellular digestion and recycling.
5. (B) Rough ER makes proteins for export, such as digestive enzymes. Detoxification (C) is a smooth ER job; starch storage (A) and photosynthesis (D) happen elsewhere.
6. (D) Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole. Both cell types have mitochondria, ribosomes, a nucleus, a plasma membrane, ER, and Golgi.

Topic 2.2

1. (C) Cristae increase the inner membrane’s surface area, so more electron transport chains and ATP synthase enzymes fit, and more ATP can be made.
2. (A) Secreted proteins are made on the rough ER, travel in a transport vesicle to the Golgi, and leave in a secretory vesicle that fuses with the plasma membrane.
3. (D) Smooth ER contains enzymes that detoxify drugs and alcohol; liver cells increase their smooth ER when exposed to these chemicals.
4. (B) Without the enzyme, the lipid cannot be broken down, so it builds up inside lysosomes. This accumulation damages nerve cells.
5. (C) The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma. The thylakoid membranes (A) hold the light-dependent reactions.
6. (D) Without enough water, the central vacuoles shrink, turgor pressure falls, and the nonwoody parts of the plant droop.

Topic 2.3

1. (B) SA = 6 × 32 = 54 µm2; V = 33 = 27 µm3; 54 ÷ 27 = 2 : 1. (Shortcut: 6 ÷ 3 = 2.)
2. (C) Cube 5 µm: 6 ÷ 5 = 1.2. Sphere r = 4: 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. Cube 20: 0.3. Sphere r = 10: 0.3. The smallest cube has the largest ratio.
3. (D) Microvilli increase membrane surface area for absorption without much increase in volume.
4. (A) Volume grows with the cube of length and surface area with the square, so SA:V falls as a cell grows, and exchange can no longer keep up. (B) and (D) reverse the relationship.
5. (C) The base diffuses the same distance into every cube, so that layer is the largest fraction of the smallest cube, which has the highest SA:V.
6. (B) A high SA:V ratio means a small animal loses heat quickly relative to the heat its body volume produces, so it must have a higher metabolic rate per gram.

Topic 2.4

1. (A) Phospholipids form a bilayer with hydrophilic heads facing water and hydrophobic tails facing each other.
2. (C) The interior of the bilayer is hydrophobic, so the membrane-spanning region has nonpolar R groups that interact favorably with the fatty acid tails.
3. (B) Cholesterol buffers fluidity: it restrains movement when warm and prevents tight packing when cold.
4. (D) Carbohydrate chains on glycoproteins (and glycolipids) act as identification tags for cell–cell recognition.
5. (A) Mixing of the labels shows that membrane proteins drift sideways within the fluid bilayer. The experiment says nothing about flipping (C) or sequences (D).
6. (D) Peripheral proteins sit on the surface and do not enter the hydrophobic core; integral proteins are embedded.

Topic 2.5

1. (B) O2 is small and nonpolar, so it dissolves in the hydrophobic interior and crosses rapidly.
2. (D) Charged ions are repelled by the hydrophobic core. Size (A) is not the reason; K+ is smaller than many molecules that cross.
3. (A) Steroids are nonpolar lipids, so they pass directly through the bilayer.
4. (C) Glucose is large and polar, so cells use carrier proteins (such as GLUT transporters).
5. (B) K+ is charged and essentially cannot cross a pure bilayer. CO2, O2, and small steroids are nonpolar.
6. (D) The porous cell wall gives support; the selectively permeable plasma membrane controls what enters the cytoplasm.

Topic 2.6

1. (C) Passive transport moves molecules down their gradient with no metabolic energy.
2. (A) At equilibrium, molecules keep moving randomly, but movement in every direction is balanced, so there is no net movement.
3. (D) A steeper concentration gradient increases the rate of diffusion. The other choices would slow it.
4. (B) K+ is moving from 5 mM to 140 mM, against its gradient, which requires active transport.
5. (A) The sodium–potassium pump uses ATP directly. Diffusion and osmosis (B, C, D) need no ATP.
6. (D) Oxygen moves from high to low concentration with no energy input. (A) and (C) move ions against gradients; (B) is phagocytosis, which uses energy.

Topic 2.7

1. (B) Aquaporins are water channels that allow rapid osmosis.
2. (C) A plateau shows a limited number of carriers that become saturated. Simple diffusion (A) would keep increasing.
3. (A) Each cycle moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in and uses 1 ATP.
4. (D) Facilitated diffusion is passive: down the gradient, through a protein, no ATP. Active transport also uses proteins (B).
5. (B) Na+ is more concentrated outside and the inside is negative, so Na+ flows in through the open channels.
6. (C) The pump removes more positive charge than it brings in, and K+ leaks out, leaving the inside negative.

Topic 2.8

1. (B) Distilled water is hypotonic to red blood cells, so water enters; with no cell wall, they swell and may lyse.
2. (D) The salt solution is hypertonic, so water leaves and the cell plasmolyzes. Salt does not move “by osmosis” (C).
3. (A) ΨS = −(1)(0.2)(0.0831)(300) = −4.99 ≈ −5.0 bars. Remember to convert 27°C to 300 K.
4. (C) Water moves from higher Ψ (−0.3 MPa, the solution) to lower Ψ (−0.6 MPa, the cell).
5. (D) In an isotonic solution, there is no net water gain, so the contractile vacuole has less water to remove.
6. (B) Freshwater fish constantly gain water and lose salt, so they excrete dilute urine and actively absorb salt at the gills. (A) describes saltwater fish.

Topic 2.9

1. (A) Engulfing a large particle with pseudopodia is phagocytosis.
2. (C) The cotransporter couples Na+ moving down its gradient to glucose moving up its gradient (symport, secondary active transport).
3. (D) Vesicles fusing with the plasma membrane and releasing their contents is exocytosis.
4. (B) Without functional receptors, cells cannot take up LDL by receptor-mediated endocytosis, so LDL cholesterol stays in the blood.
5. (A) The cotransporter depends on the H+ gradient. Without the pumps, the gradient dissipates and sucrose uptake falls. (C) ignores that the gradient comes from ATP-powered pumps.
6. (D) Bulk transport and pumps need energy. The processes in (A), (B), and (C) are passive.

Topic 2.10

1. (B) Separate compartments let incompatible processes run at the same time under their own conditions.
2. (D) The membrane keeps the acidic interior, and the enzymes, separate from the cytosol.
3. (A) In prokaryotes, the electron transport chain is embedded in the plasma membrane.
4. (C) Separating transcription from translation allows mRNA to be processed (and checked) before translation.
5. (A) Producing and destroying H2O2 in the same compartment keeps the toxic molecule away from the rest of the cell.
6. (C) Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes with internal thylakoid membranes formed from the plasma membrane, but no membrane-bound organelles. They have ribosomes (D).

Topic 2.11

1. (C) Mitochondria and chloroplasts descended from prokaryotes engulfed by a host cell.
2. (A) Circular DNA resembles a bacterial chromosome. The other choices are true of many structures and do not point to a bacterial origin.
3. (B) Mitochondrial ribosomes respond to an antibacterial drug like bacterial ribosomes do, which supports a bacterial origin.
4. (D) A feature shared by almost all members of a group was most likely present in their common ancestor; a feature found in only one lineage was acquired later.
5. (A) Engulfment wraps the bacterium in a piece of the host’s membrane, producing two membranes.
6. (C) The endomembrane system and nuclear envelope most likely formed from infoldings of the plasma membrane.

Think About It: Diagram B has a cell wall, chloroplasts, a large central vacuole, and plasmodesmata. The wall gives support and lets the cell become turgid without bursting; chloroplasts let plants make their own food by photosynthesis; the central vacuole stores water and maintains turgor; plasmodesmata connect cells through their walls. Diagram A has a centrosome with centrioles (and lysosomes), which plant cells lack.

Diagram C · The Plasma Membrane

1. Glycoprotein (carbohydrate chain on a protein) · 2. Glycolipid · 3. Channel protein · 4. Integral (transmembrane) protein · 5. Peripheral protein · 6. Cytoskeleton fibers · 7. Cholesterol · 8. Hydrophobic fatty acid tails (interior of the bilayer)

Short Answer 1: Structure 4 (and the channel protein, 3). The middle of a transmembrane protein lies in the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer, so it contains nonpolar R groups that interact with the fatty acid tails.

Short Answer 2: Structures 1 and 2. Their carbohydrate chains serve as identification tags.

Short Answer 3: Structure 3, the channel protein. Region 8 is hydrophobic, and a charged ion such as K+ is repelled by it.

Section II: Free Response — Scoring Guidelines

Question 1 (9 points)

(a) 1 pt. A bilayer of phospholipids, with hydrophilic heads facing the water on each side and hydrophobic tails inside, in which proteins are embedded or attached and can move laterally (the lipids and proteins form a fluid mosaic).
(b) 1 pt. Independent: temperature. Dependent: absorbance (amount of pigment leaked). Constant (one): size of beet cylinders, time in water, volume of water, beet source, rinsing method.
(c) 2 pts. 1 pt for correctly labeled axes with units (x: temperature, °C; y: absorbance) and appropriate scale. 1 pt for correctly plotted points with error bars (±2 SE).
(d) 1 pt. Pigment leakage increases with temperature, slowly from 0°C to 40°C and sharply above 40°C; the largest change is between 50°C and 60°C (0.35 to 0.82). The error bars for 20°C (0.07–0.09) and 40°C (0.10–0.14) do not overlap, so the difference is likely significant.
(e) 2 pts. 1 pt: high temperature increases the movement of phospholipids, making the bilayer more fluid and creating gaps, so the membrane becomes more permeable. 1 pt: high temperature breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold membrane proteins in shape, so proteins denature and leave openings in the membrane.
(f) 2 pts. 1 pt: prediction—absorbance will be higher than in the 20°C water treatment. 1 pt: justification—ethanol dissolves the phospholipids of the tonoplast and plasma membrane, disrupting the barrier, so more pigment escapes.

Question 2 (8 points)

(a) 1 pt. Ribosomes on the rough ER synthesize the protein and thread it into the ER lumen, where it folds and begins to be modified before being sent to the Golgi in vesicles.
(b) 2 pts. 1 pt: the proteins move from the rough ER to the Golgi complex to secretory vesicles. 1 pt: evidence—radioactivity is highest in the rough ER at 3 minutes (86%), peaks in the Golgi at 20 minutes (43%), and is highest in secretory vesicles at 40 and 120 minutes (62% and 75%).
(c) 1 pt. (43 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = 437.5% increase (accept 438%).
(d) 2 pts. 1 pt: prediction—the percentage in the rough ER at 40 minutes will be higher than 24%. 1 pt: justification—proteins can leave the ER in vesicles, but the vesicles cannot deliver them to the Golgi, so radioactivity stays in the ER and transport vesicles instead of moving on to the Golgi and secretory vesicles.
(e) 2 pts. 1 pt: prediction—the protein will remain in the cytosol (it will not be secreted). 1 pt: justification—without the signal sequence, the ribosome is not directed to the rough ER, so the protein is made by a free ribosome and never enters the endomembrane system.

Question 3 (4 points)

(a) 1 pt. Cell X: SA = 600 µm2, V = 1000 µm3, SA:V = 0.6. Cell Y: SA = 2(40×5) + 2(40×5) + 2(5×5) = 850 µm2, V = 1000 µm3, SA:V = 0.85. Both must be correct.
(b) 1 pt. Cell Y, because it has a higher SA:V ratio, so it has more membrane available for absorbing nutrients per unit of volume.
(c) 1 pt. If the side doubled to 20 µm, volume would increase 8 times but surface area only 4 times, so SA:V would fall to 0.3; the membrane could not take in nutrients and remove wastes fast enough for the larger volume.
(d) 1 pt. Folding the plasma membrane into microvilli (or other projections or folds) increases surface area without increasing volume.

(b) 1 pt. Any two: double membrane; their own circular DNA; their own bacteria-like ribosomes; reproduction by binary fission; size similar to bacteria; DNA sequences similar to bacteria.
