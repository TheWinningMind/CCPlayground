TOPIC 2.3

Cell Size

Learning Targets
  • Calculate the surface area, volume, and surface area-to-volume ratio of cubes, spheres, and other simple shapes.
  • Explain why the surface area-to-volume ratio limits how large a cell can be.
  • Describe structural adaptations that increase surface area in cells and organisms.
  • Explain how surface area-to-volume ratios affect heat exchange in organisms.

Why Are Cells So Small?

A cell must constantly exchange materials with its surroundings. Oxygen and nutrients come in; carbon dioxide and wastes go out. All of this traffic passes through the plasma membrane, so the rate of exchange depends on the cell’s surface area. Meanwhile, the amount of material the cell needs, and the amount of waste it produces, depends on how much cytoplasm it has to support, which is its volume.

Here is the problem: as an object gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area. Surface area increases with the square of the length, but volume increases with the cube of the length. So as a cell grows, each unit of membrane must serve more and more cytoplasm. Eventually the membrane cannot bring in materials or remove wastes fast enough, and diffusion inside the cell becomes too slow to reach the center. This is why most cells are microscopic, and why a cell divides rather than growing forever.

Worked Example

A spherical cell has a radius of 3 µm. Calculate its surface area-to-volume ratio.

SA = 4πr2 = 4 × 3.14 × 32 = 113 µm2.  V = (4/3)πr3 = (4/3) × 3.14 × 33 = 113 µm3.  SA:V = 113 / 113 = 1 : 1 (shortcut: 3 / r = 3 / 3 = 1).

If the radius doubles to 6 µm, SA:V = 3 / 6 = 0.5 : 1. The bigger cell has only half as much membrane for each cubic micrometer of cytoplasm. More practice is in the Unit 2 Math Toolkit.

Adaptations That Increase Surface Area

Cells and organisms that depend on rapid exchange have evolved shapes and structures that increase surface area without greatly increasing volume:

  • Microvilli on the cells lining the small intestine are tiny finger-like projections of the plasma membrane that greatly increase the area for absorbing nutrients.
  • Root hair cells in plants have long, thin extensions that increase the area for absorbing water and minerals.
  • Flattened or elongated shapes, such as the thin cells lining the alveoli of the lungs or the long, thin axons of neurons, keep every part of the cell close to the membrane. Red blood cells are flattened, biconcave disks, which increases their surface area for gas exchange.
  • Internal membrane folds, such as mitochondrial cristae and chloroplast thylakoids (Topic 2.2), apply the same principle inside the cell.
  • Organ-level structures, such as alveoli in lungs, gill filaments in fish, and the villi of the intestine, increase surface area for the whole organism.

Surface Area, Volume, and Heat

The same geometry affects how quickly organisms gain or lose heat. Heat is exchanged across the body surface, but it is produced throughout the body’s volume. A small animal such as a shrew has a high SA:V ratio, so it loses body heat quickly. To stay warm, it must have a very high metabolic rate per gram of body mass and must eat almost constantly. A large animal such as an elephant has a low SA:V ratio, loses heat slowly, and has a much lower metabolic rate per gram. Elephants’ large, thin ears increase their surface area and help them shed excess heat.

Misconception Alert

A bigger cell has more surface area, but a smaller SA:V ratio. Students often say a large cell exchanges materials poorly because it has “less surface area.” That is wrong: it has more surface area in absolute terms. The problem is that it has less surface area relative to its volume. Always use the word “ratio” or say “relative to its volume.”

Exam Tip

A full-credit explanation of cell size has three links: (1) as a cell grows, volume increases faster than surface area, (2) so the SA:V ratio decreases, (3) so the membrane cannot exchange materials (nutrients, O2, wastes, or heat) quickly enough to meet the needs of the cell’s volume.

Plasma Membranes

Learning Targets
  • Describe the structure of the plasma membrane using the fluid mosaic model.
  • Explain how the amphipathic nature of phospholipids causes them to form a bilayer.
  • Describe how membrane proteins are positioned in the bilayer according to the polarity of their amino acids.
  • Explain the roles of cholesterol, glycoproteins, and glycolipids in membranes.

Phospholipids Build the Bilayer

Recall from Unit 1 that a phospholipid has a hydrophilic (polar) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (nonpolar) fatty acid tails. A molecule with both regions is amphipathic. In water, phospholipids spontaneously arrange themselves into a bilayer: the hydrophilic heads face the watery environments inside and outside the cell, and the hydrophobic tails point toward each other in the interior, away from water. No energy input is needed; the bilayer forms because of hydrophobic interactions.

The Fluid Mosaic Model

The accepted model of membrane structure is the fluid mosaic model. The membrane is fluid because phospholipids and many proteins are not locked in place; they drift sideways within their layer, much like people moving through a crowd. It is a mosaic because many different kinds of proteins are embedded in or attached to the bilayer, like tiles in a mosaic.

Membrane Proteins

Proteins give each membrane its specific functions. They are classified by how they are positioned in the membrane:

  • Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer. Most are transmembrane proteins that span the entire membrane. The region inside the bilayer is made of amino acids with nonpolar (hydrophobic) R groups, often coiled into α helices, which interact favorably with the fatty acid tails. The regions exposed to water on either side have polar and charged (hydrophilic) R groups. This is a perfect example of structure determining position.
  • Peripheral proteins are not embedded in the bilayer. They are loosely bound to the surface of the membrane, often attached to the exposed parts of integral proteins or to the polar heads of phospholipids.

Membrane proteins carry out at least six major functions: transport (channels, carriers, and pumps), enzymatic activity, signal transduction (receptors that bind signaling molecules), cell–cell recognition, intercellular joining (junctions between neighboring cells), and attachment to the cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix.

Cholesterol and Membrane Fluidity

A membrane must stay fluid to work properly: if it becomes too stiff, proteins cannot move or change shape; if it becomes too fluid, it leaks. Several factors affect fluidity:

  • Temperature. As temperature drops, phospholipids move more slowly and pack more tightly, so the membrane becomes more rigid.
  • Fatty acid saturation. Unsaturated fatty acid tails have kinks from cis double bonds that prevent tight packing, so they keep membranes more fluid at low temperatures.
  • Cholesterol. In animal cell membranes, cholesterol is wedged between phospholipids. It acts as a fluidity buffer: at moderate to high temperatures it restrains phospholipid movement and reduces fluidity, and at low temperatures it keeps phospholipids from packing tightly, which prevents the membrane from solidifying.

Glycoproteins and Glycolipids

Short carbohydrate chains are attached to some membrane proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids) on the outside surface of the plasma membrane. These carbohydrates vary from species to species, individual to individual, and cell type to cell type, so they serve as identification tags for cell–cell recognition. For example, the A, B, and O human blood types are determined by differences in the carbohydrate chains on the surface of red blood cells, and immune cells use surface molecules to tell the body’s own cells from foreign ones.

Misconception Alert

“Fluid” does not mean the membrane is liquid or flows away. The bilayer is held together by hydrophobic interactions. Individual molecules move sideways within their own layer quickly, but they rarely flip from one layer to the other, because that would require dragging a hydrophilic head through the hydrophobic interior.

Exam Tip

If you are asked to predict which part of a transmembrane protein contains nonpolar amino acids, choose the region that sits in the middle of the bilayer. If a question gives you a hydropathy plot (a graph of how hydrophobic each part of a protein is), the long hydrophobic stretches of about 20 amino acids are the membrane-spanning regions.

Membrane Permeability

Learning Targets
  • Explain why the plasma membrane is selectively permeable.
  • Predict whether a molecule can cross the phospholipid bilayer directly, based on its size and polarity.
  • Explain why ions and large polar molecules require transport proteins.
  • Describe the structure and function of cell walls in plants, fungi, and prokaryotes.

Selective Permeability

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances cross easily, slows others, and blocks others completely unless a transport protein helps. This allows the cell to keep its internal environment different from the outside. Whether a molecule can cross the bilayer on its own depends mainly on two features: its polarity (or charge) and its size. The interior of the bilayer is hydrophobic, so it is a barrier to anything that is charged or strongly polar.

What Can Cross, and Why

Type of moleculeExamplesCrosses the bilayer directly?
Small nonpolarO2, CO2, N2Yes, rapidly. They dissolve in the hydrophobic interior.
Hydrophobic (lipid-soluble)Steroid hormones, fatty acidsYes. Even fairly large hydrophobic molecules pass because they are not repelled by the lipid tails.
Small polar, unchargedH2O, ethanol, glycerol, ureaSlowly. Cells that move a lot of water use aquaporins to speed it up.
Large polarGlucose, sucrose, amino acidsNo (or extremely slowly). They need carrier proteins.
Ions (charged)Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cl−, H+No. A charge, plus the hydration shell of water around it, cannot pass through the hydrophobic core. They need channels or pumps.
Misconception Alert

Small size does not guarantee passage. A hydrogen ion (H+) is tiny, yet it cannot cross the bilayer on its own because it is charged. Polarity and charge matter more than size. Also, a steroid hormone is larger than glucose, but it crosses easily because it is nonpolar.

Cell Walls

Plants, fungi, and most prokaryotes have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane. The cell wall is a rigid structural boundary that protects the cell, maintains its shape, and keeps it from bursting when water rushes in. Cell walls are made of complex carbohydrates:

  • Plants: cellulose
  • Fungi: chitin
  • Bacteria: peptidoglycan (a polymer of sugars cross-linked by short peptides)

The cell wall is porous. Water, ions, and small molecules pass through it easily, so it does not control what enters the cytoplasm; that is the plasma membrane’s job. Cell walls can, however, block very large molecules and pathogens. In plants, channels called plasmodesmata pass through the walls of neighboring cells and connect their cytoplasm.

Exam Tip

When asked whether a molecule can cross the membrane without help, ask two questions in order: (1) Is it charged? If yes, it needs a protein. (2) Is it nonpolar or small? If it is nonpolar, it crosses. If it is large and polar, it needs a protein. Justify with the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.
