Science Practices and Free-Response Strategy

The AP Biology Exam has two sections: multiple choice and free response. In recent years the free-response section has included two long questions (one of which involves interpreting an experiment) and four short questions that target specific skills such as experimental design, data analysis, models, and visual representations. Always confirm the current format in the official College Board course description. Whatever the format, the scoring rewards the same habits.

Know Your Task Verbs

Every free-response prompt begins with a task verb. Each verb asks for something different, and answering the wrong verb is the fastest way to lose points.

VerbWhat it asks forUnit 2 example of a full-credit response
IdentifyName or state the answer. No explanation needed.“Identify the organelle that packages proteins for secretion.” → The Golgi complex.
DescribeGive the relevant characteristics of something.“Describe the structure of a mitochondrion.” → A smooth outer membrane and a folded inner membrane (cristae) surrounding a fluid matrix.
ExplainGive the how or why; connect cause and effect.“Explain why cells are small.” → As size increases, volume increases faster than surface area, so the membrane cannot exchange materials fast enough to supply the cell.
PredictState an expected outcome. A reason is not required unless asked.“Predict what happens to a plant cell in 1 M NaCl.” → It loses water and becomes plasmolyzed.
JustifyGive evidence or reasoning that supports a claim or prediction.“Justify your prediction.” → The solution has a lower water potential than the cell, so water moves out of the cell by osmosis.
CalculatePerform a mathematical calculation and show work.“Calculate the solute potential of 0.2 M sucrose at 27°C.” → −(1)(0.2)(0.0831)(300) = −4.99 bars.
Construct / RepresentDraw a graph, table, or diagram.Plot data with a labeled x-axis (independent variable), labeled y-axis (dependent variable), units, a title, and a key if needed.
Support / RefuteUse evidence to argue for or against a claim.“Mitochondria divide by binary fission and have circular DNA, which supports the claim that they descended from bacteria.”

Experimental Design Vocabulary

Independent variable
The factor the experimenter deliberately changes (x-axis). Example: sucrose molarity.
Dependent variable
The factor that is measured in response (y-axis). Example: percent change in mass of potato cores.
Controlled variables (constants)
Conditions kept the same in all groups so they cannot explain differences in results: core size, temperature, soaking time.
Negative control
A group in which no response is expected; shows what happens without the treatment.
Positive control
A group in which a known response is expected; shows the method can detect an effect.
Null hypothesis
The prediction that the independent variable has no effect.
Percent change
(Final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. Use it when samples start at different sizes.
Error bars
Often ±2 SEx̄ (about 95% confidence). If error bars of two groups do not overlap, the difference is likely significant; if they overlap a lot, it is likely not.

Five Rules for Free-Response Success

  1. Answer in order and label your parts. Readers score each part separately. Write (a), (b), (c) and keep each answer next to its label.
  2. Be specific. Say “the hydrophobic interior of the phospholipid bilayer repels the charged ion,” not “the membrane blocks it.”
  3. Use “because.” For every explain or justify prompt, make sure your sentence contains a cause and an effect.
  4. Use the data. When a graph or table is given, quote actual numbers with units.
  5. Don’t contradict yourself. Readers do not award credit for a correct answer buried among incorrect ones. Give one clear answer.
The Claim–Evidence–Reasoning Template

Claim: State your answer in one sentence. Evidence: Point to the specific data or structural feature. Reasoning: Explain the biological principle that links the evidence to the claim. Example: “Claim: The potato cells have a water potential of about −7.8 bars. Evidence: The cores neither gained nor lost mass in about 0.32 M sucrose. Reasoning: No net water movement means the water potential inside the cells equals the water potential of that solution.”
