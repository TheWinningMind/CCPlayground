TOPIC 2.8

Tonicity and Osmoregulation

Learning Targets
  • Define osmosis and predict the direction of water movement between two solutions.
  • Use the terms hypertonic, hypotonic, and isotonic correctly, and predict what happens to animal and plant cells in each.
  • Calculate solute potential and water potential and use them to predict water movement.
  • Explain how organisms osmoregulate.

Step 1: Osmosis

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from the side with a lower solute concentration (more free water) to the side with a higher solute concentration (less free water). Put simply: water follows solutes. The membrane must be permeable to water but not to the solute; otherwise the solute itself would diffuse and even things out.

Step 2: Tonicity

Tonicity describes how a surrounding solution affects a cell’s water content. The terms always compare two solutions, so always ask: “hypertonic relative to what?”

Step 3: Water Potential

Biologists predict the direction of water movement precisely using water potential (symbol Ψ, the Greek letter psi). Water potential measures the tendency of water to move. Its unit is the bar or the megapascal (1 MPa = 10 bars). The single rule to remember is:

Water always moves from higher water potential to lower water potential.

Pure water at atmospheric pressure has Ψ = 0. Adding solute lowers water potential (makes it negative). Adding pressure raises it.

Worked Example 1 · Solute Potential of a Sucrose Solution

Problem: Calculate the solute potential of a 0.20 M sucrose solution at 22°C in an open beaker.

  1. List the variables. i = 1.0 (sucrose does not ionize); C = 0.20 mol/L; R = 0.0831; T = 22 + 273 = 295 K.
  2. Substitute. ΨS = −(1.0)(0.20)(0.0831)(295).
  3. Multiply. 0.20 × 0.0831 = 0.01662; 0.01662 × 295 = 4.90. So ΨS = −4.90 bars.
  4. Find Ψ. The beaker is open, so ΨP = 0 and Ψ = 0 + (−4.90) = −4.90 bars.
Worked Example 2 · Ionic Solute (NaCl)

Problem: Calculate the solute potential of a 0.10 M NaCl solution at 25°C.

  1. Variables. i = 2.0 (NaCl → Na+ + Cl−); C = 0.10; R = 0.0831; T = 298 K.
  2. Calculate. ΨS = −(2.0)(0.10)(0.0831)(298) = −4.95 bars.
  3. Notice. Because NaCl releases two particles per formula unit, a 0.10 M NaCl solution has almost the same solute potential as the 0.20 M sucrose solution above. Solute potential depends on the number of dissolved particles.
Worked Example 3 · Which Way Will Water Move?

Problem: A plant cell has ΨS = −7.0 bars and ΨP = +3.0 bars. It is placed in a beaker of solution with Ψ = −2.0 bars. Which way does water move?

  1. Cell’s water potential. Ψ = ΨP + ΨS = 3.0 + (−7.0) = −4.0 bars.
  2. Compare. The solution is −2.0 bars; the cell is −4.0 bars. −2.0 is higher (less negative) than −4.0.
  3. Apply the rule. Water moves from higher to lower Ψ, so water moves into the cell.
  4. Predict. As water enters, the cell becomes more turgid and ΨP rises until the cell’s Ψ equals −2.0 bars. Then there is no net movement.

Step 4: Reading an Osmosis Experiment

A classic lab places pieces of potato in sucrose solutions of different molarities and measures the percent change in mass. Percent change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100. A gain in mass means the solution was hypotonic to the potato; a loss means it was hypertonic. The concentration at which the graph crosses 0% change is isotonic to the potato cells—it estimates the potato’s solute concentration.

Step 5: Osmoregulation

Osmoregulation is the control of water balance and solute concentration in an organism. Examples:

  • Freshwater protists such as Paramecium live in hypotonic water, so water constantly enters. A contractile vacuole collects excess water and pumps it out, using energy, to prevent bursting.
  • Plants rely on hypotonic surroundings to stay turgid; a wilting plant has lost turgor pressure.
  • Freshwater fish excrete large amounts of dilute urine and actively take up salt through their gills; saltwater fish drink seawater and actively excrete salt.
  • In humans, the kidneys regulate the solute concentration of the blood, and IV fluids must be isotonic to blood cells.
Misconception Alert

Salt does not “suck” water, and solutes do not move in osmosis—water does. Also, with negative numbers, “higher” means closer to zero: −2 bars is higher than −5 bars. Many students reverse this and lose the point.

Exam Tip

For any osmosis question, write the comparison explicitly: “The solution has a higher solute concentration (lower water potential) than the cell, so water moves out of the cell by osmosis.” Naming both solute concentration and water potential shows full understanding.

Key Terms
Osmosis
Diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
Tonicity
The ability of a surrounding solution to cause a cell to gain or lose water.
Hypertonic
Having a higher solute concentration than another solution.
Hypotonic
Having a lower solute concentration than another solution.
Isotonic
Having an equal solute concentration.
Plasmolysis
Shrinking of a plant cell’s cytoplasm away from its wall in a hypertonic solution.
Turgid / flaccid
Firm from water pressure against the wall / limp from lack of it.
Water potential (Ψ)
Measure of the tendency of water to move; water moves from high to low Ψ.
Solute potential (ΨS)
Component of Ψ due to dissolved solutes; always zero or negative.
Pressure potential (ΨP)
Component of Ψ due to physical pressure.
Osmoregulation
Control of water balance and solute concentration.
Contractile vacuole
Organelle that pumps excess water out of freshwater protists.
