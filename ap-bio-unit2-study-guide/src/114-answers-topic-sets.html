Answers: Topic Practice Sets

Topic 2.1 · Cell Structure and Function

1. (B) Ribosomes are non-membranous complexes composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins. mRNA serves as a template during protein synthesis but is not a permanent structural component of the ribosome. Distractor analysis: The temporary association of mRNA with a ribosome does not make mRNA one of its defining structural components.
2. (C) The endomembrane system includes ER, Golgi complex, nuclear envelope, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, and plasma membrane. These components cooperate in modifying, packaging, and transporting cellular products. Distractor analysis: Mitochondria and chloroplasts are double-membrane organelles but are not components of the endomembrane system.
3. (A) Ribosomes associated with rough ER translate polypeptides as they enter the ER compartment en route to secretion. Translation typically begins on a cytosolic ribosome; a targeting signal can direct the ribosome–nascent-chain complex to rough ER, where translation continues. Because the stem specifies entry into rough ER, choice A is the best answer. Distractor analysis: Free ribosomes synthesize many cytosolic proteins, but the ribosome must become ER-associated for the secretory polypeptide to enter the ER during translation.
4. (D) Golgi processing can chemically modify proteins, including modifications that affect function or where a protein is delivered. Translation, ATP generation, and plant water storage occur elsewhere. Distractor analysis: Synthesis of a polypeptide and its entry into rough ER precede the Golgi-dependent modification and sorting stages.
5. (C) Mitochondria are the site of aerobic cellular respiration, which produces most of the cell’s ATP. Modification and sorting occur in the Golgi complex, hydrolysis in lysosomes, and lipid synthesis in the smooth ER.
6. (B) Chloroplasts are characteristic of plants and photosynthetic algae and are the sites of photosynthesis in those organisms. Distractor analysis: Photosynthetic bacteria such as cyanobacteria carry out photosynthesis without chloroplasts.
7. (B) A membrane network without attached ribosomes is consistent with smooth ER, which supports lipid synthesis and detoxification.
8. (D) The plasma membrane can receive materials delivered by vesicles, contributing to exchange and trafficking between cellular compartments and the cell exterior.

Topic 2.2 · Cell Size

1. (C) The cube has area 6(1²) = 6 cm² and volume 1³ = 1 cm³, so SA:V = 6:1.
2. (A) A rectangular solid has two faces of each area lw, lh, and wh.
3. (D) Area has units length² and volume has units length³, so their ratio has units 1/length.
4. (C) Flattening an equal-volume shape generally increases its surface area, while a sphere minimizes area for a fixed volume.
5. (C) Smaller bodies have a larger exposed-area-to-volume ratio, allowing more proportional heat exchange under comparable conditions.
6. (C) Extra membrane area increases surface area while volume is approximately constant.
7. (B) The ratios are 6/2=3 μm⁻¹ and 6/4=1.5 μm⁻¹.
8. (D) The CED identifies a typical inverse relationship between size and metabolic rate per unit body mass. This is a broad trend, not an absolute rule.

Topic 2.3 · Plasma Membrane

1. (B) The phosphate-containing head is polar and faces aqueous surroundings. Choice A identifies hydrophobic chains, not the water-contacting polar head; C describes the nonpolar membrane center; D is a protein region, not part of a phospholipid.
2. (A) Phospholipids contain polar hydrophilic heads and nonpolar hydrophobic tails. B, C, and D do not define molecules containing both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions; amphipathicity concerns compatibility with water, not merely charge.
3. (C) Cytosol-exposed protein surfaces can interact with water through polar/charged regions. A and B would make the protein poorly compatible with cytosolic water; D confuses extracellular carbohydrate decoration with core protein chemistry.
4. (D) A glycoprotein is a protein with covalently attached carbohydrate groups. A describes a phospholipid; B a steroid; C a lipid with no attached carbohydrate; only D has a carbohydrate linked to protein.
5. (A) The nonpolar fatty acid tails create a hydrophobic bilayer interior. B and D refer to aqueous environments; C refers to polar heads. The hydrocarbon tail region is the hydrophobic environment.
6. (B) The arrangement reverses the hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails. A, C, and D discuss other properties rather than the reversed placement of phospholipid head/tail regions.
7. (C) Mosaic refers to varied components associated with the phospholipid framework. A ignores molecular diversity; B mistakes the membrane for an organelle; D reverses the location of the carbohydrate and bilayer molecules.
8. (B) The inner leaflet faces the aqueous cytosol. The inner leaflet faces cytosol, not outer leaflet tails, bilayer center, or extracellular glycoconjugates.

Topic 2.4 · Membrane Permeability

1. (A) The hydrophobic core imposes an energetic barrier to charged and many polar substances; the other features alone do not explain selectivity.
2. (B) Both CO₂ and N₂ are small, nonpolar molecules. Water is polar; glucose is relatively large and polar; sodium is charged.
3. (C) Molecular polarity, not the presence of oxygen atoms, predicts whether CO₂ crosses the lipid interior readily. Common pitfall: A molecule with polar bonds can still be nonpolar overall, as in CO₂.
4. (C) Wall rigidity opposes expansion and helps prevent osmotic lysis; it does not prevent all water movement. Common pitfall: The protective wall does not make the plasma membrane impermeable to water.
5. (A) Even when size is comparable, charge strongly reduces passage through a hydrophobic bilayer. Common pitfall: A similarly sized ion is not comparable to a nonpolar molecule in lipid solubility.
6. (B) A compatible channel provides an aqueous route across the otherwise unfavorable hydrophobic core.
7. (A) Walls differ in composition but can provide structural support and restrict some movement, limiting osmotic lysis.
8. (B) Comparing lysis frequency under matched conditions isolates the predicted effect of wall integrity.

Topic 2.5 · Membrane Transport

1. (A) Selective permeability can limit solute exchange, permitting unequal concentrations. A gradient does not require synthesis of solute or a single obligatory direction of motion.
2. (D) Net entry from 8 mM to 36 mM is against the concentration gradient and is consistent with active transport. The observation alone should not specify a particular pump.
3. (B) Membrane fusion connects the vesicle lumen with the extracellular environment. Inward folding and vesicle formation describe endocytosis.
4. (B) Passive net transport proceeds from higher to lower concentration across a permeable boundary. A higher internal concentration initially favors net export.
5. (B) Exocytosis releases cargo by vesicle fusion at the plasma membrane. The other processes involve inward uptake from outside the cell.
6. (D) Gradient magnitude is characterized by the concentration difference between the compartments. A smaller difference directly indicates a weaker gradient under a simple concentration model.
7. (C) A reagent that quenches only external fluorescence helps separate internalized particles from surface-bound ones. Total cell-associated signal alone can overestimate uptake.
8. (A) Active transport can establish or maintain a gradient, which can later drive passive movement. Passive movement does not itself begin consuming ATP when a separate active process stops.

Topic 2.9 · Cell Compartmentalization

1. (A) The double-membraned nuclear envelope delimits a specialized nuclear environment. Ribosomes and centrosomes are important structures, but they do not enclose the nucleoplasm.
2. (D) A limiting membrane helps maintain distinct molecular composition and conditions; it does not exclude all water. Enzyme concentration without a separating lipid boundary does not alone create a membrane-bounded compartment.
3. (A) A lipid-bilayer boundary defines membrane-bounded compartments; nonmembranous complexes can also be highly functional. Proteins and enzymatic complexes can function without an enclosing bilayer; not all organelles are double membrane bound.
4. (C) Compartmentation is a potential advantage, not a guarantee of higher throughput if other factors limit metabolism. Compartment number alone does not establish substrate supply, catalytic ability or pathway coordination.
5. (A) Cristae expand inner membrane area; their specific ATP synthesis roles are explored more deeply in Unit 3. Cristae are folds of the inner, not outer, mitochondrial membrane.
6. (B) A distinct lumen can support specialized conditions while the cytosol maintains different conditions. Compartmentation permits different local conditions but does not eliminate controlled molecular exchange.
7. (B) Functional compartments balance separation with selective traffic, not total impermeability. Selective traffic and compartment boundaries coexist; neither perfect sealing nor abolition of enzymes is implied.
8. (C) The official scope identifies both reduction of competing interactions and increased reaction area. Both separation of competing processes and area expansion are supported; none removes the need for exchange or substrates.
