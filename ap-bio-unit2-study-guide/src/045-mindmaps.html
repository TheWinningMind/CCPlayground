Mind Map 1 · Organelles and the Endomembrane System

All Cells Have

  • Plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes
  • Prokaryotes: no nucleus; DNA in nucleoid; plasmids; no membrane-bound organelles; 1–5 µm
  • Eukaryotes: nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; 10–100 µm

Nucleus & Ribosomes

  • Nucleus: holds DNA; double nuclear envelope with pores; nucleolus makes rRNA
  • Ribosomes: rRNA + protein; make proteins; free (cytosolic proteins) or bound to rough ER (secreted and membrane proteins)
  • Shared by all life → evidence of common ancestry

Endoplasmic Reticulum

  • Rough ER: ribosomes; makes and compartmentalizes proteins for export
  • Smooth ER: lipid synthesis, detoxification, Ca2+ storage
  • Continuous with the nuclear envelope; mechanical support and transport

Golgi Complex

  • Stack of cisternae; cis face receives, trans face ships
  • Folds, modifies (adds carbohydrates), sorts, and packages proteins into vesicles
  • Does not make proteins
Organellesstructure fits
function

Secretory Pathway

  • Ribosome on rough ER → ER lumen → transport vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane → exocytosis
  • Membrane proteins follow the same path

Lysosomes & Vacuoles

  • Lysosome: hydrolytic enzymes at pH 5; digests food, autophagy, apoptosis
  • Central vacuole (plants): water storage, turgor pressure, pigments, wastes
  • Contractile vacuole: pumps water out of freshwater protists

Mitochondria

  • Double membrane; folded inner membrane (cristae) → more surface area for ATP synthesis
  • Krebs cycle in the matrix; electron transport on the inner membrane
  • Abundant in muscle and sperm cells

Chloroplasts

  • Double membrane; thylakoids stacked in grana; fluid stroma
  • Light reactions on thylakoid membranes; Calvin cycle in stroma
  • Plants and algae only
  • Plants also have a cellulose cell wall
Ask of every organelle: What is it made of? What does it do? Which cells have it?

Mind Map 2 · The Plasma Membrane

Phospholipid Bilayer

  • Phospholipids are amphipathic: hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails
  • Heads face water inside and outside; tails face each other in the interior
  • Forms spontaneously by hydrophobic interactions

Fluid Mosaic Model

  • Fluid: lipids and proteins drift sideways within each layer
  • Mosaic: many different proteins embedded in the bilayer
  • Evidence: fused mouse–human cells mix their membrane proteins

Membrane Proteins

  • Integral: embedded; transmembrane proteins have nonpolar amino acids in the bilayer and polar ones at the surfaces
  • Peripheral: loosely bound to the surface
  • Jobs: transport, enzymes, receptors, recognition, junctions, attachment

Fluidity

  • Higher temperature → more fluid
  • Unsaturated (kinked) tails → more fluid
  • Cholesterol: fluidity buffer; restrains movement when warm, prevents packing when cold

Cross Freely

  • Small nonpolar: O2, CO2, N2
  • Hydrophobic: steroid hormones, fatty acids
  • Small polar (slowly): H2O, ethanol, urea

Need Proteins

  • Ions of any size: charged, repelled by the hydrophobic core
  • Large polar molecules: glucose, amino acids
  • Lots of water: aquaporins

Carbohydrate Markers

  • Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer surface only
  • Cell–cell recognition: blood types, immune “self” markers

Cell Walls

  • Outside the membrane; rigid but porous
  • Plants: cellulose · Fungi: chitin · Bacteria: peptidoglycan
  • Structural support; prevents bursting
  • Plasmodesmata connect plant cells
The hydrophobic interior is the barrier; proteins are the gates.

Mind Map 3 · Membrane Transport

Gradients

  • A concentration difference across a membrane stores potential energy
  • Down the gradient: high → low, releases energy
  • Against the gradient: low → high, requires energy

Simple Diffusion

  • Passive; no protein; no energy
  • Small nonpolar molecules
  • Faster with steeper gradient, higher temperature, more surface area, shorter distance
  • Ends at dynamic equilibrium (no net movement)

Facilitated Diffusion

  • Passive; uses a protein; no energy
  • Channels: ions (often gated); aquaporins for water
  • Carriers: bind and change shape (GLUT for glucose)
  • Rate levels off when carriers are saturated

Primary Active Transport

  • Pumps use ATP directly to move solutes against gradients
  • Na+/K+ ATPase: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP
  • Proton pump: H+ out (plants, fungi, bacteria)
Membrane Transportpassive vs.
active

Membrane Potential

  • Inside negative (about −70 mV in neurons)
  • Pump moves more + out than in; K+ leaks out
  • Electrochemical gradient = concentration + charge

Secondary Active Transport

  • Cotransporter uses one solute’s gradient to move another uphill
  • Symport (same direction): Na+–glucose, H+–sucrose
  • Antiport (opposite directions): Na+/Ca2+ exchanger
  • Stops if the pump stops

Exocytosis

  • Vesicle fuses with plasma membrane, releases contents
  • Insulin, digestive enzymes, neurotransmitters
  • Requires energy

Endocytosis

  • Phagocytosis: engulfs large particles (macrophages, amoebas)
  • Pinocytosis: nonspecific uptake of fluid
  • Receptor-mediated: specific ligands, coated pits (LDL)
  • Requires energy
Which direction? Protein needed? Energy needed? Those three questions classify any transport.

Mind Map 4 · Osmosis and Water Potential

Osmosis

  • Diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane
  • Water moves toward the side with more solute
  • Passive; often through aquaporins

Tonicity (outside vs. inside)

  • Hypertonic: more solute outside → water leaves cell
  • Hypotonic: less solute outside → water enters cell
  • Isotonic: equal → no net movement

Animal Cells

  • Hypotonic: swell and may lyse (burst)
  • Isotonic: normal (ideal)
  • Hypertonic: shrivel (crenation)

Plant Cells

  • Hypotonic: turgid (ideal; wall prevents bursting)
  • Isotonic: flaccid (limp)
  • Hypertonic: plasmolyzed (membrane pulls from wall)
Water PotentialΨ = ΨP + ΨS
high Ψ → low Ψ

Water Potential (Ψ)

  • Water always moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ
  • Pure water, open container: Ψ = 0
  • Units: bars or MPa (1 MPa = 10 bars)

Solute Potential (ΨS)

  • ΨS = −iCRT; always 0 or negative
  • i: 1 for sucrose, 2 for NaCl
  • R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K; T = °C + 273
  • More solute → more negative → lower Ψ

Pressure Potential (ΨP)

  • 0 in an open container
  • Positive in a turgid plant cell (wall pushes back)
  • Raises Ψ and slows further water entry

Osmoregulation

  • Paramecium: contractile vacuole pumps out water
  • Freshwater fish: dilute urine, take up salt at gills
  • Saltwater fish: drink seawater, excrete salt at gills
  • Humans: kidneys and aquaporins; IV fluids are isotonic
Water follows solute. A more negative water potential pulls water in.

Mind Map 5 · Compartmentalization and Its Origins

Why Compartments?

  • Different reactions at the same time without interference
  • Specific conditions in each compartment (pH, enzymes)
  • Harmful reactions kept apart
  • More membrane surface area for reactions

Examples

  • Lysosome: pH 5 for hydrolytic enzymes
  • Peroxisome: H2O2 and catalase kept together
  • Nucleus: transcription separated from translation
  • Mitochondrion: H+ gradient across inner membrane

Prokaryotes

  • No membrane-bound organelles
  • Plasma membrane holds electron transport chains
  • Some have membrane infoldings (cyanobacteria thylakoids)
  • High SA:V lets the plasma membrane serve the whole cell

Membrane Infolding

  • Origin of the nuclear envelope and endomembrane system
  • Explains why the nuclear envelope is continuous with the ER

Endosymbiotic Theory

  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by a host cell
  • Relationship became mutually beneficial and permanent

Order of Events

  • First: aerobic proteobacterium → mitochondrion (ancestor of all eukaryotes)
  • Later: cyanobacterium → chloroplast (algae and plants only)

Evidence

  • Double membrane (inner = bacterial; outer = host)
  • Own circular DNA
  • Own bacteria-like ribosomes (antibiotic sensitive)
  • Divide by binary fission
  • Bacterial size; DNA sequences match bacteria

Key AP Connections

  • Unit 3: respiration and photosynthesis in these organelles
  • Unit 7: evidence of common ancestry
  • Most organelle genes moved to the nucleus
Compartments let a large cell stay organized; endosymbiosis explains where two of them came from.
