TOPIC 2.3

Cell Size

Learning Targets
  • Calculate the surface area, volume, and surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) of cubes, spheres, and cylinders.
  • Explain why a high SA:V ratio makes exchange with the environment more efficient.
  • Explain how cells and organisms increase surface area (microvilli, root hairs, folding, flattening).
  • Explain how SA:V affects heat exchange in organisms of different sizes.

Why Are Cells So Small?

Every cell must exchange materials with its environment: nutrients and oxygen must come in, and wastes and heat must go out. All of this exchange happens across the plasma membrane—the cell’s surface area. But the amount of material a cell needs, and the waste it produces, depends on how much cytoplasm it has—its volume.

Here is the problem: as a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area. Volume grows with the cube of the length (L3), but surface area grows only with the square (L2). A large cell would have too little membrane to supply all of its cytoplasm, and substances would have to diffuse a long way to reach the center. So cells stay small, and a high SA:V ratio means more efficient exchange.

Worked Example 1 · Comparing Two Cubes

Problem: Cell A is a cube 2 µm on each side. Cell B is a cube 4 µm on each side. Which cell exchanges materials more efficiently?

  1. Surface area. A: 6 × 22 = 6 × 4 = 24 µm2. B: 6 × 42 = 6 × 16 = 96 µm2.
  2. Volume. A: 23 = 8 µm3. B: 43 = 64 µm3.
  3. Ratio (divide SA by V). A: 24 ÷ 8 = 3 (3 : 1). B: 96 ÷ 64 = 1.5 (1.5 : 1).
  4. Interpret. Cell A has twice as much membrane per unit volume, so it exchanges materials more efficiently. Doubling the side length halved the SA:V ratio.
Worked Example 2 · A Spherical Cell

Problem: A spherical cell has a radius of 3 µm. Calculate its SA:V ratio. (Use π ≈ 3.14.)

  1. Surface area. 4 × 3.14 × 32 = 4 × 3.14 × 9 = 113.04 µm2.
  2. Volume. (4/3) × 3.14 × 33 = (4/3) × 3.14 × 27 = 113.04 µm3.
  3. Ratio. 113.04 ÷ 113.04 = 1 (1 : 1).
  4. Shortcut. For any sphere, SA:V = 3/r. Here 3 ÷ 3 = 1. For a cube, SA:V = 6/s. These shortcuts show directly that the ratio falls as size increases.

How Cells and Organisms Increase Surface Area

When a cell or organ needs a lot of exchange, it can increase surface area without greatly increasing volume:

  • Microvilli — finger-like projections of the plasma membrane on intestinal cells greatly increase the surface area for absorbing nutrients.
  • Root hairs — long, thin extensions of root epidermal cells increase the area for absorbing water and minerals.
  • Folded internal membranes — cristae and thylakoids (Topic 2.2).
  • Flattened or elongated shapes — a flat or long, thin cell has a higher SA:V ratio than a sphere of the same volume. Red blood cells are flattened disks; neurons are long and thin.
  • Organ-level structures — the millions of alveoli in the lungs and the branched filaments of fish gills.

SA:V and Heat Exchange

The same math applies to whole organisms. Heat is produced by the volume of the body and lost through its surface. A small animal, such as a shrew, has a high SA:V ratio, so it loses heat rapidly and must have a high metabolic rate per gram to stay warm. A large animal, such as an elephant, has a low SA:V ratio, retains heat well, and has a lower metabolic rate per gram. Large animals in hot climates often have structures that add surface area for cooling, such as an elephant’s large, thin ears.

Misconception Alert

A larger cell has a larger surface area but a smaller SA:V ratio. Students often say “bigger cells have less surface area.” That is false. The problem is that surface area does not keep up with volume. Always talk about the ratio.

Exam Tip

When asked to explain a size limit, give the full chain: as size increases → volume increases faster than surface area → SA:V decreases → the membrane cannot exchange materials (or heat) fast enough to meet the needs of the volume. Answers that stop at “the cell is too big” do not earn the point.

Key Terms
Surface area
The total area of the outside of an object; for a cell, the area of the plasma membrane.
Volume
The amount of space an object occupies; for a cell, the amount of cytoplasm.
SA:V ratio
Surface area divided by volume; higher values mean more efficient exchange.
Microvilli
Membrane projections that increase the surface area of absorptive cells.
