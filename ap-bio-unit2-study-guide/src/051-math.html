The Unit 2 Math Toolkit

Unit 2 includes two calculations that appear on the AP Biology Exam again and again: surface area-to-volume ratios and water potential. Both formulas are printed on the exam’s formula sheet, so the challenge is not memorizing them but setting up the problem correctly and interpreting the answer. Work through the examples, then try the practice problems. Answers with full working are in Part 4. A calculator is allowed on the exam.

Part A · Surface Area and Volume

The Steps
  1. Identify the shape and write the formulas for SA and V.
  2. Substitute the measurements, keeping the units (µm2 for area, µm3 for volume).
  3. Divide SA by V. Express the result as a ratio (for example, 3 : 1) or a single number with units of µm−1.
  4. Interpret: the larger the ratio, the more membrane per unit of cytoplasm, and the faster the cell can exchange materials relative to its needs.
Worked Example A1 · Rectangular Cell

A cell is shaped like a box 8 µm long, 2 µm wide, and 2 µm high. Find its SA:V ratio and compare it with a cube of the same volume.

SA = 2lw + 2lh + 2wh = 2(8×2) + 2(8×2) + 2(2×2) = 32 + 32 + 8 = 72 µm2. V = 8 × 2 × 2 = 32 µm3. SA:V = 72 ÷ 32 = 2.25 : 1.

A cube with V = 32 µm3 has s = ∛32 ≈ 3.17 µm, so SA:V = 6 ÷ 3.17 ≈ 1.89 : 1. The elongated cell has more membrane for the same volume. This is why many cells that specialize in exchange are long and thin.

Practice Problems A

  1. Calculate the SA:V ratio of a cube-shaped cell with sides 4 µm long.
  2. A cube of agar has sides 0.5 mm long. Calculate its surface area, volume, and SA:V ratio.
  3. Calculate the surface area, volume, and SA:V ratio of a spherical cell with a radius of 2 µm. (Use π = 3.14.)
  4. A rectangular cell measures 10 µm × 2 µm × 2 µm. Calculate its SA:V ratio.
  5. A cylindrical bacterium has a radius of 1 µm and a length of 10 µm. Calculate its SA:V ratio.
  6. Cell A is a cube with 2 µm sides. Cell B is a sphere with a radius of 1.5 µm. Which cell can exchange materials with its environment more efficiently? Justify your answer with calculations.

Part B · Water Potential

The Steps
  1. Find the solute potential: ΨS = −iCRT. Use i = 1 for sugars (sucrose, glucose) and i = 2 for NaCl. Convert temperature: K = °C + 273. R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K.
  2. Find the pressure potential ΨP. It is 0 for an open container and usually positive in a turgid plant cell.
  3. Add them: Ψ = ΨP + ΨS.
  4. Compare: water moves from the higher (less negative) Ψ to the lower (more negative) Ψ.
Worked Example B1 · Salt Solution

Calculate the water potential of a 0.1 M NaCl solution in an open beaker at 27°C.

NaCl splits into two ions, so i = 2. T = 27 + 273 = 300 K. ΨS = −(2)(0.1)(0.0831)(300) = −4.99 bars. The beaker is open, so ΨP = 0 and Ψ = −4.99 bars. Notice that 0.1 M NaCl has the same effect on water as 0.2 M sucrose, because it releases twice as many dissolved particles.

Worked Example B2 · A Plant Cell

A plant cell has a solute potential of −7.0 bars and a pressure potential of +3.0 bars. It is placed in a beaker of solution with a water potential of −2.0 bars. Which way will water move?

Ψcell = ΨP + ΨS = 3.0 + (−7.0) = −4.0 bars. The solution (−2.0 bars) has the higher water potential, so water moves into the cell. As water enters, the cell’s pressure potential rises until the two water potentials are equal.

Practice Problems B

  1. Calculate the solute potential of a 0.3 M sucrose solution at 20°C.
  2. Calculate the solute potential of a 0.15 M NaCl solution at 25°C.
  3. A plant cell has ΨS = −6.0 bars and ΨP = +2.5 bars. Calculate its water potential. If the cell is placed in a solution with Ψ = −5.0 bars, predict the direction of water movement.
  4. Potato cores placed in a 0.25 M sucrose solution at 22°C neither gain nor lose mass. Calculate the water potential of the potato cells.
  5. Convert a water potential of −0.8 MPa to bars.
  6. A membrane permeable only to water separates 0.2 M glucose (side A) from 0.1 M NaCl (side B) at the same temperature. Predict the net movement of water and justify your prediction.
The Five Most Common Math Mistakes
  • Forgetting to convert °C to kelvin (add 273).
  • Using i = 1 for NaCl. Salts that ionize have i > 1.
  • Dropping the negative sign in ΨS = −iCRT.
  • Thinking −8 bars is “higher” than −3 bars. It is lower.
  • Saying a bigger cell has less surface area. It has more surface area but a smaller SA:V ratio.
