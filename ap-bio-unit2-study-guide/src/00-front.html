How to Use This Guide

Unit 2 moves biology from molecules to the cell. The chemistry of Unit 1—polarity, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and the structure of proteins and phospholipids—now explains how real cells are built and how they control what goes in and out. Students who can connect a cell structure to the job it does find Units 3 through 6 much easier, because respiration, photosynthesis, cell signaling, and gene expression all happen in the organelles and membranes you meet here.

This guide covers all eleven topics of AP Biology Unit 2. Each topic follows the same pattern so you always know where to look:

  • Learning Targets — what you should be able to do by the end of the lesson. Use them as a checklist.
  • The Lesson — a clear, step-by-step explanation with 3D illustrations, diagrams, and tables.
  • Worked Examples — the Unit 2 calculations (surface area-to-volume ratio and water potential) solved one step at a time.
  • Misconception Alerts — the mistakes that cost students points year after year.
  • Exam Tips — how the idea tends to be tested and how to phrase strong answers.
  • Key Terms — the vocabulary you must be able to use precisely.
  • Check Your Understanding — exam-style multiple-choice questions. Full explanations are in Part 4.

Topic 2.1 opens with a 3D Organelle Gallery: one card per structure showing what it looks like, where it is found, and what it does. After the lessons, three mind maps summarize the unit on single pages, and the Who Am I? Vocabulary Challenge in Part 3 gives you 100 quick clues to sharpen your vocabulary.

Study Habits That Work

  • Retrieve, don’t reread. Close the book and list every organelle and its function, then check. Testing yourself is far more effective than highlighting.
  • Draw it. If you can sketch an animal cell, a plant cell, the fluid mosaic membrane, and cells in hypotonic and hypertonic solutions from memory, you understand Unit 2.
  • Always name the direction. For every transport question, say which way the substance moves relative to its concentration gradient and whether energy is used.
  • Explain the “because.” AP questions rarely ask what an organelle is; they ask why its structure suits its function and what would happen if it failed.
  • Space it out. Return to earlier topics every few days. Short, repeated reviews beat one long cram session.

Unit 2 at a Glance

On the AP Biology Exam, Unit 2 has historically made up roughly 10–13% of the multiple-choice score. Its ideas—membranes, gradients, compartments—are the foundation for cellular energetics (Unit 3) and cell communication (Unit 4), so its true weight on the exam is larger than that number suggests.

The One Big Idea

Membranes create boundaries, and boundaries make life possible. The plasma membrane separates the cell from its surroundings so the cell can keep a different internal environment. Internal membranes divide eukaryotic cells into compartments where different reactions can run at the same time without interfering. Because membranes are selectively permeable, cells can build up concentration gradients—and gradients store energy that cells use to make ATP, send signals, and move substances. On almost every Unit 2 free-response question, the winning answer connects a specific structure (a membrane, a protein, a fold, a compartment) to a specific function.
