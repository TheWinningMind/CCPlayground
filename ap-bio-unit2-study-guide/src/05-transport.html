TOPIC 2.6

Membrane Transport

Learning Targets
  • Describe a concentration gradient and explain why it stores energy.
  • Compare passive and active transport in terms of direction and energy.
  • Explain how selective permeability lets cells maintain concentration gradients.
  • Describe endocytosis and exocytosis as ways to move large substances.

Step 1: Concentration Gradients

A concentration gradient exists when a substance is more concentrated in one region than another. Molecules are always in random motion, so over time they spread out from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. This net movement is diffusion, and it continues until the concentration is equal everywhere (dynamic equilibrium—molecules still move, but there is no net change).

Moving “down” a gradient (high → low) happens on its own. Moving “up” a gradient (low → high) requires energy. Because a gradient is an uneven arrangement that would naturally even out, a gradient is a form of stored potential energy. Cells spend energy to build gradients and then use them to power work.

Step 3: Moving Large Substances

Some molecules, such as proteins and polysaccharides, are far too large for any channel or carrier. Cells move them in bulk using vesicles. Both processes require energy.

  • Exocytosis — a vesicle inside the cell fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside. Example: secretion of insulin or neurotransmitters.
  • Endocytosis — the plasma membrane folds inward around material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell. Example: a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium. Topic 2.9 describes the three kinds of endocytosis.
Misconception Alert

“Passive” does not mean “without proteins.” Facilitated diffusion uses proteins and is still passive, because the substance moves down its gradient and the cell spends no energy. The test for passive vs. active is direction and energy, not whether a protein is involved.

Key Terms
Concentration gradient
A difference in concentration of a substance between two regions.
Diffusion
Net movement of a substance from higher to lower concentration.
Dynamic equilibrium
State with no net change although molecules continue to move.
Passive transport
Movement down a gradient with no energy input from the cell.
Active transport
Movement against a gradient using energy, through a carrier protein.
Endocytosis
Uptake of material by folding in the plasma membrane to form a vesicle.
Exocytosis
Release of material when a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane.

TOPIC 2.7

Facilitated Diffusion

Learning Targets
  • Explain how channel proteins and carrier proteins move polar molecules and ions down their gradients.
  • Describe aquaporins and gated ion channels.
  • Explain why the rate of facilitated diffusion levels off (saturates) at high concentrations.

Two Kinds of Helper Proteins

Facilitated diffusion is the passive movement of a substance down its concentration gradient with the help of a transport protein. It moves polar molecules and ions that cannot easily cross the hydrophobic core. Each transport protein is specific: its shape and the chemistry of its interior allow only certain substances to pass.

  • Channel proteins form a hydrophilic tunnel through the membrane. Substances flow through rapidly. Examples:
    • Aquaporins — channels that let water molecules pass in single file, thousands of times faster than through the bilayer. Kidney cells and plant root cells have many.
    • Ion channels — let specific ions pass. Many are gated: they open or close in response to a signal, such as a change in voltage or the binding of a molecule. Gated channels are essential for nerve impulses and muscle contraction (Unit 4).
  • Carrier proteins bind a specific substance, change shape, and release it on the other side. They are slower than channels. Example: GLUT glucose transporters move glucose into cells down its gradient.

Rate of Facilitated Diffusion

The rate of simple diffusion keeps increasing as the concentration gradient gets steeper. The rate of facilitated diffusion also increases at first, but then levels off. Why? There are a limited number of transport proteins. Once every carrier is busy (the carriers are saturated), adding more solute cannot speed things up. The only way to raise the maximum rate is to add more transport proteins to the membrane.

Exam Tip

A graph that levels off as concentration increases is the signature of a protein-mediated process (facilitated diffusion or active transport). If a question asks how to increase the maximum rate, the answer is more transport proteins, not a bigger gradient.

Misconception Alert

Facilitated diffusion can never move a substance against its gradient. No matter how many channels or carriers there are, the substance still only moves from high to low concentration. To go against the gradient, the cell needs active transport.

Key Terms
Facilitated diffusion
Passive movement of a substance down its gradient through a transport protein.
Channel protein
Protein with a hydrophilic pore that substances flow through.
Carrier protein
Protein that binds a substance and changes shape to move it across.
Gated channel
A channel that opens or closes in response to a stimulus.
Saturation
Point at which all transport proteins are in use and the rate cannot increase.
Specificity
A transport protein’s ability to move only particular substances.
