TOPIC 2.10

Cell Compartmentalization

Learning Targets
  • Explain how internal membranes allow different reactions to occur in different compartments at the same time.
  • Explain how compartmentalization increases efficiency and protects the cell.
  • Describe how membrane folding increases surface area for reactions.

Why Divide the Cell into Rooms?

A house has a kitchen, a bedroom, and a bathroom so that different activities can happen at once without getting in each other’s way. Eukaryotic cells work the same way. Internal membranes divide the cell into compartments, each with its own chemical conditions. This compartmentalization has four main benefits:

  1. Separate, even opposite, reactions can run at the same time. Fatty acids can be built in the cytosol while they are broken down in peroxisomes and mitochondria.
  2. Each compartment keeps its own ideal conditions. Lysosomes keep a pH of about 5 for their enzymes, while the cytosol stays near pH 7.2.
  3. Enzymes and substrates are concentrated. Keeping all the enzymes for one pathway in one small space speeds up reactions.
  4. Harmful substances are isolated. Hydrolytic enzymes stay in lysosomes; hydrogen peroxide is made and destroyed inside peroxisomes.

Membranes as Work Surfaces

Membranes don’t just separate compartments; they also hold the enzymes and transport proteins for many reactions. Membranes can also maintain gradients between compartments—for example, a high concentration of H+ in the space between the two mitochondrial membranes—which cells use to make ATP (Unit 3). Folding (cristae, thylakoids, the rough ER) packs a large membrane area into a small volume, increasing the number of reactions that can occur.

Misconception Alert

Prokaryotes are not “less evolved” because they lack compartments. Prokaryotes are extremely successful. Their small size gives them a high SA:V ratio, and some use infoldings of the plasma membrane for specialized reactions. Compartmentalization is one solution to the problem of organizing a cell, especially a large one.

Key Terms
Compartmentalization
Division of the cell interior into membrane-bound regions with different functions.
Intermembrane space
Region between the inner and outer membranes of a mitochondrion or chloroplast.

Origins of Cell Compartmentalization

Learning Targets
  • Describe the endosymbiotic theory, step by step.
  • Explain the evidence that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes.
  • Describe how the endomembrane system may have evolved by infolding of the plasma membrane.

The Endosymbiont Theory

Where did membrane-bound organelles come from? The endosymbiont theory (endosymbiotic theory) proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by a larger host cell. Instead of being digested, they survived inside it, and the two cells came to depend on each other. An endosymbiont is an organism that lives inside the cells of another.

Step by Step

  1. Infolding. An ancestral prokaryotic cell’s plasma membrane folded inward. These folds pinched off to form the nuclear envelope and the endomembrane system (ER and Golgi).
  2. First engulfment. This early eukaryotic cell engulfed an aerobic (oxygen-using) prokaryote but did not digest it.
  3. Mutual benefit. The engulfed cell provided ATP from aerobic respiration; the host provided protection and nutrients. Over many generations, the endosymbiont became the mitochondrion. All eukaryotes descend from this cell.
  4. Second engulfment. Later, some of these cells engulfed a photosynthetic prokaryote (related to cyanobacteria), which became the chloroplast. These cells gave rise to algae and plants.

The Evidence

Mitochondria and chloroplasts share many features with bacteria that other organelles lack:

EvidenceWhy it supports endosymbiosis
Double membraneThe inner membrane matches the original prokaryote’s plasma membrane; the outer membrane came from the host’s engulfing vesicle.
Own circular DNALike a bacterial chromosome, and unlike the linear chromosomes in the nucleus.
Own ribosomes, similar in size to bacterial ribosomesThey make some of the organelle’s own proteins, and they are sensitive to some antibiotics that affect bacterial ribosomes.
Reproduce by a process like binary fissionThey divide on their own inside the cell, the way bacteria do; the cell cannot make them from scratch.
Similar size to bacteriaMitochondria are about the size of a typical bacterium.
DNA sequence comparisonsMitochondrial DNA is most similar to that of a group of aerobic bacteria; chloroplast DNA is most similar to that of cyanobacteria.
Exam Tip

On a free-response question about endosymbiosis, you are often asked for evidence. Each piece of evidence earns credit only if you explain the comparison: not just “mitochondria have DNA,” but “mitochondria have their own circular DNA, like that of bacteria, which suggests they were once independent prokaryotes.”

Misconception Alert

Mitochondria today cannot live on their own. Over time, many of the endosymbiont’s genes moved to the host’s nucleus, so mitochondria depend on proteins made by the cell. Also, the double membrane of the nucleus is explained by infolding, not endosymbiosis.

Key Terms
Endosymbiont theory
Theory that mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from prokaryotes engulfed by a host cell.
Endosymbiont
An organism that lives inside another organism’s cells.
Binary fission
Prokaryotic cell division by splitting in two.
Infolding
Inward folding of the plasma membrane, thought to have formed the endomembrane system.
