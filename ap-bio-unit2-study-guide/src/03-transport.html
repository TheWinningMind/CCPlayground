TOPIC 2.6

Membrane Transport

Learning Targets
  • Describe how a concentration gradient forms across a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Distinguish between passive and active transport.
  • Explain diffusion and dynamic equilibrium, and identify factors that affect the rate of diffusion.
  • Describe how endocytosis and exocytosis move large substances using energy.

Concentration Gradients

Because the plasma membrane is selectively permeable, the concentration of a substance can be different on the two sides. A difference in concentration across a space is called a concentration gradient. Gradients are a form of stored (potential) energy, much like water held behind a dam. Molecules tend to move down their gradient, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated, releasing that energy. Moving molecules up (against) their gradient requires an input of energy.

Diffusion and Equilibrium

All molecules are in constant random motion because of their thermal (kinetic) energy. Diffusion is the net movement of molecules from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration as a result of this random motion. No energy from the cell is needed. Diffusion continues until the concentration is the same everywhere, a state called dynamic equilibrium. At equilibrium, molecules still move back and forth across the membrane, but at equal rates in both directions, so there is no net movement.

The rate of diffusion increases when:

  • the concentration gradient is steeper (a bigger difference in concentration),
  • the temperature is higher (molecules move faster),
  • the surface area available for exchange is larger,
  • the distance the molecules must travel is shorter, and
  • the molecules are smaller (and, for crossing a bilayer, nonpolar).

Passive and Active Transport

Every way of crossing a membrane falls into one of two categories:

  • Passive transport is the net movement of molecules down their concentration gradient, from high to low concentration, without the direct input of metabolic energy. It includes simple diffusion through the bilayer, facilitated diffusion through proteins (Topic 2.7), and osmosis (Topic 2.8).
  • Active transport moves molecules against their concentration gradient, from low to high concentration. It requires metabolic energy, usually from ATP, and it always requires a membrane protein. Active transport is how cells build and maintain gradients.

Moving Large Things: Bulk Transport

Some substances, such as proteins, polysaccharides, and whole bacteria, are far too large to pass through any membrane protein. Cells move these by enclosing them in membrane: exocytosis releases materials from the cell when a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, and endocytosis takes materials in when the plasma membrane folds inward and pinches off a vesicle. Both processes require energy. You will study their types in Topic 2.9.

Misconception Alert

Molecules do not stop moving at equilibrium. At equilibrium, there is no net movement, because molecules cross in both directions at the same rate. Also, molecules do not “want” to spread out or “know” where the low concentration is. Diffusion is simply the statistical result of random motion.

Exam Tip

To classify any transport, ask two questions: Which direction relative to the gradient? (Down = passive; against = active.) Is energy from the cell used? (No = passive; yes = active.) The use of a protein alone does not make transport active: facilitated diffusion uses proteins but is passive.

Diffusion
Net movement of molecules from high to low concentration.
Dynamic equilibrium
Equal movement in both directions; no net change.
Passive transport
Movement down a gradient with no metabolic energy input.
Active transport

Bulk transport
Movement of large materials in vesicles (endocytosis, exocytosis).

TOPIC 2.7

Facilitated Diffusion

Learning Targets
  • Explain why charged and large polar molecules need membrane proteins to cross a membrane.
  • Compare channel proteins, aquaporins, and carrier proteins.
  • Explain why the rate of facilitated diffusion levels off at high solute concentrations.
  • Describe how the Na+/K+ ATPase uses ATP to maintain gradients and the membrane potential.

Proteins Provide a Path

Facilitated diffusion is the passive movement of molecules down their concentration gradient through a transport protein. It is still passive transport: the protein only provides a hydrophilic route through the membrane, and the energy comes from the gradient itself. Facilitated diffusion is how cells move charged ions and large polar molecules that cannot pass through the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.

Channel Proteins

Channel proteins form hydrophilic tunnels through the membrane. They are highly specific: a potassium channel lets K+ through but not Na+. Many are gated channels that open or close in response to a signal, such as a change in voltage or the binding of a molecule. In nerve cells, gated Na+ and K+ channels open in sequence to produce nerve impulses.

Aquaporins are channel proteins specialized for water. Water can leak slowly through the bilayer, but aquaporins allow large quantities of water to cross rapidly. Kidney cells that reabsorb water and plant root cells have many aquaporins.

Carrier Proteins

Carrier proteins bind a specific molecule, change shape, and release the molecule on the other side of the membrane. The glucose transporters (GLUT proteins) that let glucose enter red blood cells and muscle cells are carriers. Because each carrier must bind, change shape, and reset, carriers move molecules more slowly than channels.

Misconception Alert

Facilitated diffusion is passive. Students often assume that any process involving a protein needs energy. It does not. If molecules move down their gradient through a protein without ATP, it is facilitated diffusion, which is passive transport.

Active Transport Proteins

Active transport also requires membrane proteins, but these proteins, often called pumps, use energy to move substances against their gradients. Metabolic energy, usually from ATP, is needed both to establish concentration gradients and to maintain them, because ions constantly leak back down their gradients through channels.

The Sodium–Potassium Pump

The most important pump in animal cells is the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase). In each cycle it:

  1. binds 3 Na+ from the cytoplasm,
  2. uses one ATP to phosphorylate itself, which changes its shape and releases the 3 Na+ outside the cell,
  3. binds 2 K+ from outside, and
  4. releases the phosphate, returns to its original shape, and releases the 2 K+ inside the cell.

Because the pump moves three positive charges out for every two it brings in, and because K+ also leaks out through open K+ channels, the inside of the cell becomes negatively charged relative to the outside. This voltage difference across the membrane is the membrane potential, typically about −70 millivolts in a resting neuron. In other words, the movement of ions across the membrane makes it polarized. The combination of a concentration gradient and a charge difference is called an electrochemical gradient, and it is the energy source for nerve impulses and for many forms of cotransport (Topic 2.9).

Exam Tip

Know the numbers: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in, 1 ATP. If a question says a drug inhibits the Na+/K+ pump, predict that the Na+ and K+ gradients will gradually run down as ions leak through channels, and the membrane potential will become less negative.

TOPIC 2.8

Tonicity and Osmoregulation

Learning Targets
  • Explain osmosis and predict the direction of water movement between solutions.
  • Use the terms hypertonic, hypotonic, and isotonic correctly, and predict their effects on animal and plant cells.
  • Calculate solute potential and water potential and use them to predict water movement.
  • Describe how organisms regulate water balance (osmoregulation).

Osmosis

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from the solution with the lower solute concentration (more free water) to the solution with the higher solute concentration (less free water). Dissolved solutes bind some water molecules in hydration shells, so a concentrated solution has fewer free water molecules available to cross the membrane. Osmosis is passive; it often happens through aquaporins.

Tonicity

Tonicity describes how a solution affects a cell’s water balance. It always compares two solutions: the solution outside the cell relative to the inside.

  • Hypertonic solution: higher solute concentration than the cell. Water leaves the cell.
  • Hypotonic solution: lower solute concentration than the cell. Water enters the cell.
  • Isotonic solution: equal solute concentration. No net water movement.
Misconception Alert

“Hypertonic” is always relative. A solution is not hypertonic on its own; it is hypertonic to something. Also, remember that water follows solute: water moves toward the hypertonic side. A helpful memory aid: “hypo” sounds like “hippo,” and a cell in a hypotonic solution swells up like a hippo.

Water Potential

Biologists predict the direction of water movement using water potential (Ψ), the potential energy of water in a system. Water always moves from higher water potential to lower water potential. Water potential is measured in bars or megapascals (1 MPa = 10 bars). Pure water in an open container has a water potential of zero. Water potential has two components:

Water Potential Equations (given on the AP formula sheet)

Ψ = ΨP + ΨS    ΨS = −iCRT

  • ΨP (pressure potential) is the physical pressure on the water. In an open beaker it is 0. In a turgid plant cell, the wall pushes back and ΨP is positive.
  • ΨS (solute potential) is always zero or negative. Adding solute makes it more negative, which lowers water potential.
  • i = ionization constant (1.0 for sucrose and glucose, which do not ionize; 2.0 for NaCl, which splits into Na+ and Cl−)
  • C = molar concentration (mol/L); R = pressure constant = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K; T = temperature in kelvin (°C + 273)
Worked Example

Calculate the solute potential of a 0.1 M sucrose solution at 22°C.

ΨS = −iCRT = −(1)(0.1 mol/L)(0.0831 L·bar/mol·K)(22 + 273 K) = −(1)(0.1)(0.0831)(295) = −2.45 bars.

The solution is in an open beaker, so ΨP = 0 and Ψ = 0 + (−2.45) = −2.45 bars. If a cell with Ψ = −4.0 bars is placed in this solution, water will move into the cell, from higher (−2.45) to lower (−4.0) water potential.

Osmoregulation

Osmoregulation is the control of water balance and solute concentrations in an organism. It allows organisms to live in environments that would otherwise be hypotonic or hypertonic to their cells.

  • Freshwater protists such as Paramecium live in a hypotonic environment, so water constantly enters by osmosis. A contractile vacuole collects the excess water and pumps it out of the cell, which requires energy.
  • Plants rely on hypotonic surroundings. Water enters root cells, and the resulting turgor pressure supports the plant. Their cell walls prevent bursting.
  • Freshwater fish gain water and lose salts to their hypotonic surroundings. They drink very little, produce large amounts of dilute urine, and actively take up salts through their gills.
  • Saltwater fish lose water to their hypertonic surroundings. They drink seawater, actively excrete excess salt through their gills, and produce small amounts of concentrated urine.
  • Humans keep blood plasma nearly isotonic to their cells. The kidneys adjust the amount of water reabsorbed using aquaporins. This is why hospital IV fluids are isotonic saline (about 0.9% NaCl), not pure water.
Exam Tip

On water potential questions, write the rule first: “Water moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ.” Then compare the numbers carefully. A more negative number is lower: −5 bars is lower than −2 bars. Show every step of −iCRT, including units, and convert °C to kelvin.

TOPIC 2.9

Mechanisms of Transport

Learning Targets
  • Distinguish between primary active transport and secondary active transport (cotransport).
  • Explain how cells use an existing gradient to move another substance against its gradient.
  • Describe exocytosis and three types of endocytosis: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis.
  • Select the correct transport mechanism for a given molecule and situation.

Primary and Secondary Active Transport

In primary active transport, a pump uses ATP directly to move a substance against its gradient. The Na+/K+ pump is one example. Another is the proton pump (H+ pump), which plants, fungi, and bacteria use to pump hydrogen ions out of the cell, building a H+ gradient.

In secondary active transport, also called cotransport, the cell does not use ATP directly. Instead, a cotransporter protein lets one substance flow down the gradient that a pump created, and uses the energy of that flow to drag a second substance against its gradient. ATP is still the ultimate energy source, because ATP built the first gradient.

  • Symport: both substances move in the same direction. Example: intestinal cells use the sodium–glucose cotransporter to bring glucose in along with Na+, even when glucose is more concentrated inside the cell. Plant cells use a H+/sucrose cotransporter to load sucrose into phloem.
  • Antiport: the substances move in opposite directions. Example: a Na+/Ca2+ exchanger in heart muscle uses Na+ entering to push Ca2+ out.

Bulk Transport: Exocytosis and Endocytosis

Large molecules and particles move in and out of cells inside vesicles. These processes require energy and depend on the fluidity of the membrane, which allows vesicles to bud off and fuse.

Exocytosis

In exocytosis, a vesicle moves to the plasma membrane, fuses with it, and releases its contents outside the cell. The vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane. Examples: pancreatic cells secreting insulin and digestive enzymes, nerve cells releasing neurotransmitters, and plant cells delivering cell wall materials.

Endocytosis

In endocytosis, the plasma membrane folds inward around material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell. There are three types:

  • Phagocytosis (“cell eating”): the cell extends pseudopodia around a large particle, such as a bacterium, and engulfs it in a food vacuole, which then fuses with a lysosome. Examples: white blood cells (macrophages) and amoebas.
  • Pinocytosis (“cell drinking”): the cell takes in small droplets of extracellular fluid along with whatever solutes are dissolved in it. It is nonspecific.
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis: specific molecules (ligands) bind to receptor proteins clustered in coated pits on the membrane, and only then is the vesicle formed. This lets a cell take up large quantities of a specific substance. Example: human cells take up cholesterol by receptor-mediated endocytosis of low-density lipoprotein (LDL). People with defective LDL receptors (familial hypercholesterolemia) cannot remove LDL from the blood, so their blood cholesterol is dangerously high.

Misconception Alert

Cotransport is active transport even though the cotransporter does not use ATP. The substance moved against its gradient gets its energy from the ion gradient, and the ion gradient was built by a pump that uses ATP. If the pump stops, cotransport eventually stops too.

Exam Tip

Experimental questions often add an inhibitor and ask you to predict the effect. An ATP inhibitor stops pumps, bulk transport, and (eventually) cotransport, but not simple or facilitated diffusion. A protein-destroying treatment stops facilitated diffusion, pumps, and cotransport, but not simple diffusion of small nonpolar molecules.
