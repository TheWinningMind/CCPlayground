TOPIC 2.4

Plasma Membranes

Learning Targets
  • Describe the structure of the plasma membrane using the fluid mosaic model.
  • Explain how the amphipathic nature of phospholipids causes them to form a bilayer.
  • Describe the roles of integral and peripheral proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins, and glycolipids.
  • Explain the factors that affect membrane fluidity.

Step 1: Phospholipids Form a Bilayer

Recall from Unit 1 that a phospholipid is amphipathic: it has a hydrophilic (polar) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (nonpolar) fatty acid tails. In water, phospholipids arrange themselves so that the heads face the watery environment on both sides (the cytosol inside and the extracellular fluid outside) and the tails face each other in the middle, away from water. The result is a phospholipid bilayer with a hydrophobic core. No energy is needed to build it; hydrophobic interactions drive it to form on its own.

Step 2: The Fluid Mosaic Model

The plasma membrane is described by the fluid mosaic model:

  • Fluid — the phospholipids and many proteins are not locked in place. They drift sideways within their layer, so the membrane behaves like a thin, flexible fluid rather than a rigid wall.
  • Mosaic — the membrane is a patchwork of many different molecules: phospholipids, several kinds of proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates.

Step 3: Membrane Proteins

Proteins carry out most of the membrane’s specific functions. They are classified by how they sit in the membrane:

  • Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer. Many are transmembrane proteins that span the whole membrane. The parts inside the hydrophobic core have mostly nonpolar amino acids; the parts that stick out into water have mostly polar or charged amino acids. This is Unit 1 chemistry at work.
  • Peripheral proteins are loosely attached to the surface of the membrane or to integral proteins. They are not embedded in the hydrophobic core.

Membrane proteins perform six main jobs: transport (channels, carriers, pumps), enzymatic activity, signal reception (receptors), cell–cell recognition, intercellular joining (junctions), and attachment to the cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix.

Step 4: Carbohydrates and Cholesterol

Glycoproteins (proteins with attached carbohydrate chains) and glycolipids (lipids with attached carbohydrates) are found on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. Their sugar chains act as identification tags for cell–cell recognition: the immune system uses them to tell “self” from “nonself,” and the ABO blood types are determined by them.

Cholesterol, a steroid found in animal cell membranes, acts as a fluidity buffer. At warm temperatures it restrains the movement of phospholipids, making the membrane less fluid; at cold temperatures it prevents the tails from packing tightly, keeping the membrane from becoming too rigid.

Step 5: What Controls Fluidity?

Fluidity matters because membrane proteins must be able to move and change shape to work. Organisms adjust their membranes to their environment: plants and bacteria in cold climates increase the proportion of unsaturated fatty acids in their membranes.

Beyond the Membrane: Cell Walls and the Extracellular Matrix

Plants, fungi, and most prokaryotes have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, and peptidoglycan in bacteria. Animal cells lack walls but are surrounded by an extracellular matrix (ECM) of glycoproteins such as collagen, which supports the cell and helps it communicate with its neighbors.

Misconception Alert

The membrane is not a static, solid layer. “Fluid” means the components move laterally. Also, phospholipids rarely flip from one layer to the other, so the two layers can have different compositions—for example, carbohydrate chains are only on the outer face.

TOPIC 2.5

Membrane Permeability

Learning Targets
  • Explain what it means for a membrane to be selectively permeable.
  • Predict whether a substance can diffuse directly across the bilayer based on its size, polarity, and charge.
  • Explain why polar molecules and ions need transport proteins.
  • Describe the role of the cell wall in permeability and support.

Selective Permeability

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable: some substances cross it easily, while others cross slowly or not at all without help. This property lets the cell keep its internal environment different from the outside—an essential feature of life.

Whether a substance can slip directly through the bilayer depends on the hydrophobic core. Use this step-by-step decision:

  1. Is it small and nonpolar? (O2, CO2, N2, steroid hormones, other lipids) → It dissolves in the hydrophobic core and diffuses freely.
  2. Is it small and polar? (water, ethanol, urea, glycerol) → It crosses slowly. Water crosses much faster through channel proteins called aquaporins.
  3. Is it large and polar? (glucose, amino acids) → It needs a transport protein.
  4. Is it an ion? (Na+, K+, Cl−, H+, Ca2+) → Its charge (and its shell of water molecules) keeps it out of the hydrophobic core. It needs a channel or carrier protein.
  5. Is it a macromolecule? (proteins, polysaccharides) → Too big for any protein; it crosses by endocytosis or exocytosis (Topic 2.9).

The Role of the Cell Wall

A cell wall is not selectively permeable in the way a membrane is. It is porous and lets water and small solutes pass freely. Its jobs are structural: it maintains the cell’s shape, protects it, and keeps it from bursting when water enters. The plasma membrane, just inside the wall, is still the structure that controls what enters and leaves the cell.

Exam Tip

When asked to predict whether a molecule will cross the membrane, name the property and the reason: “O2 diffuses directly across the membrane because it is small and nonpolar, so it can pass through the hydrophobic interior of the phospholipid bilayer.” For ions: “Na+ requires a channel or carrier protein because its charge prevents it from passing through the hydrophobic core.”

Key Terms
Selective permeability
Property of a membrane that allows some substances to cross more easily than others.
Aquaporin
Channel protein that allows rapid movement of water.
Transport protein
A membrane protein that helps a substance cross.
Cell wall
Rigid, porous structural layer outside the plasma membrane.
