Answers: Check Your Understanding

Topic 2.1

1. (C) Ribosomes are in all cells. Prokaryotes lack a nucleus (A), mitochondria (B), and a Golgi complex (D).
2. (C) A wall plus a central vacuole means a plant cell; root cells are underground and lack chloroplasts. Bacteria (A) have no nucleus; animal cells (B, D) have no wall.
3. (A) Smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies. The Golgi does not make ATP (B), lysosomes digest (C), and the nucleolus assembles ribosomes (D).
4. (B) Lysosomal hydrolytic enzymes digest engulfed material and worn-out organelles.
5. (B) Ribosomes made of rRNA and protein are universal, reflecting common ancestry. Prokaryotes have no nucleus (A) or chloroplasts (C), and the cell types differ in size (D).
6. (D) Chloroplasts make sugar; mitochondria break it down to make ATP. Plants need both.

Topic 2.2

1. (B) Secreted proteins go rough ER → transport vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane.
2. (A) Making and secreting large amounts of protein requires extensive rough ER and Golgi.
3. (D) Cristae increase the inner membrane area that holds electron transport proteins and ATP synthase.
4. (A) Enzymes that leak into the near-neutral cytosol work poorly, protecting the cell from self-digestion.
5. (B) Vesicles from the ER cannot deliver their proteins to the Golgi, so those proteins are not processed or secreted. Free-ribosome proteins (A) were never in this pathway.
6. (A) Smooth ER detoxifies drugs and poisons, especially in liver cells.

Topic 2.3

1. (B) SA = 6 × 25 = 150 µm2; V = 125 µm3; 150 ÷ 125 = 1.2. (Shortcut: 6/s = 6/5.)
2. (C) Volume grows with the cube of length, surface area with the square, so SA:V falls. Surface area still increases (A is false).
3. (B) Microvilli add membrane area for absorption with little added volume.
4. (D) The smallest animal has the highest SA:V, loses heat fastest, and must produce the most heat per gram.
5. (C) A long, thin shape has the most surface per unit volume; a sphere has the least.
6. (A) The smallest cube has the highest SA:V and the shortest distance to its center, so the greatest fraction of its volume is reached in the same time.

Topic 2.4

1. (B) Hydrophilic heads face water; hydrophobic tails cluster away from it. No covalent bonds (C) or ATP (D) are involved.
2. (C) The bilayer interior is hydrophobic, so nonpolar R groups interact favorably with it.
3. (A) Carbohydrate chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids serve as identification tags.
4. (B) Kinked unsaturated tails keep membranes fluid in the cold.
5. (D) Cholesterol is a fluidity buffer. It is not a channel (B) or receptor (C).

Topic 2.5

1. (C) O2 is small and nonpolar. Ions (A), glucose (B), and amino acids (D) need proteins.
2. (B) Steroids are lipids that dissolve in the hydrophobic core.
3. (A) Charged ions are repelled by the nonpolar interior. Size (B) is not the main issue; Na+ is tiny.
4. (C) The wall is porous and structural. The plasma membrane, not the wall, controls entry (A, D).
5. (A) Without carrier proteins such as GLUT, glucose crosses a bare bilayer very slowly.

Topic 2.6

1. (C) Only active transport moves substances against their gradient with energy input. Facilitated diffusion also uses proteins (A).
2. (C) K+ is already more concentrated inside, so moving more in is against the gradient. A channel (D) would let K+ flow out.
3. (B) At dynamic equilibrium, movement continues in both directions with no net change.
4. (A) Vesicles fuse with the membrane and release their contents outside.
5. (D) An uneven distribution can do work as it evens out, like water behind a dam.

Topic 2.7

1. (B) Facilitated diffusion is passive, protein-assisted, and specific.
2. (A) Aquaporins allow rapid water movement.
3. (B) A plateau indicates saturated carrier proteins; simple diffusion (A) does not level off.
4. (C) Open channels let ions flow down their electrochemical gradient; no ATP is used.
5. (A) The maximum rate depends on the number of carriers. ATP (D) is not used in facilitated diffusion.

Topic 2.8

1. (C) Distilled water is hypotonic; water enters and the cell, lacking a wall, may lyse.
2. (A) A hypertonic solution draws water out; the membrane pulls away from the wall.
3. (B) ΨS = −(1)(0.30)(0.0831)(300) = −7.48 bars. Glucose does not ionize, so i = 1.
4. (D) −0.3 MPa is higher than −0.8 MPa, so water moves from the solution into the cell.
5. (B) Fresh water is hypotonic; without the contractile vacuole, water accumulates.
6. (C) The change in mass switches from gain to loss between 0.2 M and 0.4 M, so the isotonic point is about 0.3 M.

Topic 2.9

1. (C) Three Na+ out, two K+ in, both against their gradients.
2. (A) Phosphorylation by ATP changes the pump’s shape so it opens to the outside.
3. (D) The cotransporter depends on the Na+ gradient maintained by the pump. Once the gradient runs down, glucose uptake against its gradient fails.
4. (B) Phagocytosis forms a vesicle that fuses with a lysosome for digestion.
5. (C) LDL enters by receptor-mediated endocytosis; defective receptors block uptake.

Topic 2.10

1. (B) Compartments let reactions with different requirements happen at the same time.
2. (A) Making and destroying H2O2 in one compartment protects the rest of the cell.
3. (A) Because mRNA is made in the nucleus and translated outside it, it can be processed before translation.
4. (D) Stacked thylakoids increase the membrane area for the light reactions.

Topic 2.11

1. (A) Circular DNA like that of bacteria supports a prokaryotic origin. The other features have nothing to do with endosymbiosis.
2. (B) The inner membrane came from the engulfed cell; the outer came from the host.
3. (B) All eukaryotes have mitochondria (or descended from cells that had them), but only some have chloroplasts, so mitochondria came first.
4. (C) Mitochondrial ribosomes resemble bacterial ribosomes, so drugs that target bacterial ribosomes can affect them.
5. (D) The ER and nuclear envelope are thought to have formed by infolding; mitochondria and chloroplasts by endosymbiosis.

Section II: Scoring Guidelines

Question 1 (9 points)

(a) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Describes osmosis: the diffusion (net movement) of water across a selectively permeable membrane from an area of lower solute concentration (higher water potential) to higher solute concentration (lower water potential).
  • 1 pt — Explains the gain: 0.0 M sucrose (distilled water) is hypotonic to the potato cells (has a higher water potential), so water moved into the cells by osmosis.

(b) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Independent variable: sucrose concentration (molarity). Dependent variable: percent change in mass. (Both required.)
  • 1 pt — Percent change accounts for differences in the starting mass of the cores, so results can be compared fairly among cores.

(c) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Correctly labeled axes with units (x = sucrose concentration (M); y = % change in mass), appropriate scale including negative values, and data plotted correctly. A line or scatter plot is appropriate because concentration is continuous.
  • 1 pt — Error bars plotted correctly (e.g., +18.0 ± 1.6 spans 16.4 to 19.6).

(d) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Estimates the isotonic concentration at about 0.32 M (accept 0.30–0.35 M): the point where percent change in mass equals zero, between 0.2 M (+6.5%) and 0.4 M (−4.2%).
  • 1 pt — Calculates ΨS = −iCRT = −(1)(0.32)(0.0831)(295) ≈ −7.8 bars (accept values consistent with the estimate in the first point, about −7.4 to −8.6 bars).

(e) 1 point — Predicts that the isotonic concentration would be higher for sweet potato, because its cells have a higher solute concentration (lower solute potential), so a more concentrated sucrose solution would be needed to produce no net water movement.

Question 2 (4 points)

  • (a) 1 pt — SA = 6 × 42 = 96 µm2; V = 43 = 64 µm3; SA:V = 96 ÷ 64 = 1.5 (1.5 : 1).
  • (b) 1 pt — The 1 µm cell has a higher SA:V (6 : 1), so it has more membrane per unit of cytoplasm for exchanging nutrients and wastes, and materials diffuse a shorter distance to reach the center.
  • (c) 1 pt — Describes a feature and a matching cell type, e.g., microvilli on intestinal cells; root hairs on root epidermal cells; a long, thin shape in neurons; a flattened disk shape in red blood cells; folded membranes.
  • (d) 1 pt — The mouse. It has a higher SA:V ratio, so it loses body heat to the environment faster relative to its volume and must produce more heat (higher metabolic rate) per gram to maintain body temperature.

Question 3 (4 points)

  • (a) 1 pt — The pump uses energy from ATP to move 3 Na+ out of the cell (and 2 K+ in) against their concentration gradients, keeping Na+ concentration high outside and low inside.
  • (b) 1 pt — Identifies cotransport (secondary active transport) and explains that without Na+ outside there is no Na+ gradient to drive the cotransporter, so glucose cannot be moved against its gradient (uptake falls to 8%).
  • (c) 1 pt — Ouabain stops the pump, so Na+ that enters through the cotransporter (and leaks in) is no longer pumped out; the Na+ gradient dissipates, removing the energy source for glucose transport.
  • (d) 1 pt — At 2 minutes the existing Na+ gradient had not yet run down; it takes time for Na+ to accumulate inside the cell once pumping stops, so the cotransporter could still work.
