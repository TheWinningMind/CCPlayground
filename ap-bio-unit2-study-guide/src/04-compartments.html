TOPIC 2.10

Cell Compartmentalization

Learning Targets
  • Explain how internal membranes divide a eukaryotic cell into specialized compartments.
  • Explain how compartmentalization allows different metabolic processes to occur at the same time.
  • Explain how internal membranes increase the surface area available for reactions.
  • Compare how prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells organize their metabolism.

Why Have Compartments?

A eukaryotic cell carries out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Some of them would interfere with each other if they happened in the same place: one pathway builds a molecule while another breaks it down; some enzymes need an acidic environment, while others are destroyed by acid. Internal membranes solve this problem by dividing the cell into separate compartments, each with its own set of enzymes and its own internal conditions. This is compartmentalization.

Compartmentalization gives eukaryotic cells three main advantages:

  1. Different metabolic processes can happen at the same time without interfering with each other. For example, fatty acids can be built in the cytosol while they are broken down in mitochondria.
  2. Competing or harmful reactions are kept apart. Lysosomes hold destructive hydrolytic enzymes at pH 5, safely away from the cytosol. Peroxisomes contain the toxic by-product hydrogen peroxide and the enzyme catalase that breaks it down.
  3. Internal membranes increase surface area for reactions that happen on membranes, such as the electron transport chains of cellular respiration (on the cristae) and photosynthesis (on the thylakoids). Enzymes for the steps of a pathway can be arranged in order on the membrane, which makes the pathway faster and more efficient.

What About Prokaryotes?

Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles, yet they carry out many of the same reactions. They organize their metabolism in other ways:

  • The plasma membrane does the jobs that internal membranes do in eukaryotes. For example, the electron transport chains of aerobic bacteria are embedded in their plasma membrane.
  • Some prokaryotes have infoldings of the plasma membrane that increase surface area. Cyanobacteria, for instance, have internal thylakoid membranes where photosynthesis occurs.
  • Prokaryotic DNA is concentrated in the nucleoid region, which is not enclosed by a membrane.

Because prokaryotes are small, their high surface area-to-volume ratio lets their plasma membrane supply the whole cell. Eukaryotic cells are much larger, so their internal membranes are essential: they add surface area and organization that the plasma membrane alone could not provide.

Misconception Alert

Compartmentalization is not just about storage. On free-response questions, students often say organelles “keep things organized” and stop there. To earn credit, name the benefit: the compartment allows specific conditions (such as low pH), separates incompatible reactions, or increases surface area for a membrane-bound process.

Exam Tip

A strong answer about compartmentalization names a specific organelle, a specific condition or reaction inside it, and the specific advantage. Example: “The lysosome membrane maintains an internal pH of about 5, which allows hydrolytic enzymes to function while protecting cytosolic molecules from being digested.”

Origins of Cell Compartmentalization

Learning Targets
  • Describe the endosymbiotic theory for the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts.
  • Evaluate evidence that supports the endosymbiotic theory.
  • Explain how the endomembrane system may have evolved from infoldings of the plasma membrane.
  • Describe the order in which mitochondria and chloroplasts were acquired.

From Prokaryotes to Eukaryotes

The fossil record shows that prokaryotes existed for well over a billion years before the first eukaryotes appeared. Eukaryotic cells, with their nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, arose from prokaryotic ancestors. Two processes are thought to explain where their internal membranes came from.

1. Infolding of the Plasma Membrane

The nuclear envelope and the endomembrane system (ER, Golgi, and related membranes) probably evolved from infoldings of the plasma membrane of an ancestral prokaryote. As the membrane folded inward, it surrounded the DNA and created internal compartments. This explains why the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope is continuous with the ER.

2. Endosymbiosis

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by a larger host cell. Instead of being digested, they survived inside the host. An endosymbiont is an organism that lives inside another organism. Over many generations, the relationship became mutually beneficial and permanent:

  • An ancestral host cell engulfed an aerobic (oxygen-using) bacterium, related to today’s proteobacteria. It became the mitochondrion. The host gained a far more efficient way to make ATP, and the bacterium gained protection and a supply of nutrients.
  • Later, some cells that already had mitochondria engulfed a photosynthetic bacterium related to today’s cyanobacteria. It became the chloroplast. These cells were the ancestors of algae and plants.

Evidence for Endosymbiosis

Several independent lines of evidence support the endosymbiotic theory. Each one shows that mitochondria and chloroplasts resemble bacteria more than they resemble the rest of the eukaryotic cell:

ObservationWhy it supports endosymbiosis
Double membraneThe inner membrane matches the original bacterium’s plasma membrane; the outer membrane came from the host cell’s membrane when it engulfed the bacterium. The inner membranes contain enzymes and transport systems similar to those in bacterial plasma membranes.
Their own circular DNALike bacterial chromosomes, mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA is circular and not associated with histone proteins in the way nuclear DNA is.
Their own ribosomesTheir ribosomes are smaller than cytosolic eukaryotic ribosomes and resemble bacterial ribosomes in size, structure, and sensitivity to certain antibiotics.
Binary fissionThey reproduce by splitting in two, the way bacteria do. A cell cannot make a new mitochondrion from scratch; new ones come only from existing ones.
SizeThey are about the size of a typical bacterium (1–10 µm).
DNA sequencesComparisons of genes show that mitochondrial DNA is most closely related to that of aerobic proteobacteria, and chloroplast DNA to that of cyanobacteria.
Misconception Alert

Mitochondria cannot live on their own today. Over time, most of the original bacterium’s genes were transferred to the host’s nucleus or lost. Mitochondria and chloroplasts now depend on proteins encoded by nuclear genes. Their remaining DNA is a small leftover of the original genome, which is itself evidence of their bacterial origin.

Exam Tip

When asked for evidence of endosymbiosis, give at least two specific features and say what they resemble: “Mitochondria have their own circular DNA, like bacteria, and they reproduce by binary fission, like bacteria.” Simply saying “they have DNA” is not enough; the nucleus has DNA too.
