Structure Fits Function

Topic 2.1 asked what each organelle does. Topic 2.2 asks how its structure makes that job possible. The same idea from Unit 1 applies: structure determines function. A good answer always names a specific structural feature and then explains how that feature helps.

The Endomembrane System

The endomembrane system is a group of membranes and organelles that work together to make, modify, package, and transport proteins and lipids. It includes the nuclear envelope, rough and smooth ER, Golgi complex, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, and the plasma membrane. These parts are connected either directly (the ER is continuous with the nuclear envelope) or by vesicles, small membrane sacs that bud off one membrane and fuse with another. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not part of the endomembrane system.

Step by Step: How a Cell Makes and Secretes a Protein

Follow a digestive enzyme or a hormone such as insulin from gene to secretion:

  1. Nucleus. The gene is transcribed into mRNA, which leaves through a nuclear pore.
  2. Rough ER. A ribosome attaches to the ER and translates the mRNA. The growing polypeptide is threaded into the ER lumen, where it folds and may have sugars added.
  3. Transport vesicle. The protein is packaged into a vesicle that buds off the ER and travels to the Golgi along cytoskeleton tracks.
  4. Golgi complex. The vesicle fuses with the cis face. The protein is modified (for example, its sugar chains are trimmed or tagged), sorted, and shipped from the trans face.
  5. Secretory vesicle. A vesicle carrying the finished protein moves to the plasma membrane.
  6. Exocytosis. The vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases the protein outside the cell. The vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.
Exam Tip

A cell that secretes large amounts of protein—pancreatic cells that make digestive enzymes or insulin, plasma cells that make antibodies—has an unusually large amount of rough ER and Golgi. A cell that makes steroid hormones (cells of the ovary or testis) or detoxifies drugs (liver cells) has a large amount of smooth ER. A cell with high energy demand (muscle, sperm) is packed with mitochondria. Use this logic to predict a cell’s job from its organelles, or its organelles from its job.

Structure–Function Highlights

OrganelleKey structural featureHow it helps the function
Rough ERRibosomes on the surface; enclosed lumenProteins are made directly into a compartment where they can fold and be modified, separated from the cytosol.
Golgi complexSeparate cisternae with cis and trans facesAllows step-by-step modification in order, like an assembly line, and sorting to the correct destination.
MitochondrionDouble membrane; inner membrane folded into cristaeFolding greatly increases the surface area for electron transport chain proteins and ATP synthase, so more ATP can be made. The double membrane creates two compartments, allowing a proton gradient between them (Unit 3).
ChloroplastStacked thylakoid membranes in the stromaStacking increases the membrane surface area for light-absorbing pigments and electron transport; the thylakoid space and stroma are separate compartments for the light reactions and Calvin cycle.
LysosomeMembrane that keeps an acidic interior (pH ≈ 5)Hydrolytic enzymes work best at low pH and are kept away from the rest of the cell. If a lysosome leaks into the cytosol (pH ≈ 7.2), its enzymes are much less active, which helps protect the cell.
Central vacuoleLarge sac that takes up waterWater entering the vacuole pushes against the cell wall, creating turgor pressure that supports the plant. It also increases cell size cheaply without making more cytoplasm.
NucleusDouble membrane with poresProtects DNA while pores control traffic: mRNA and ribosome subunits exit; proteins such as enzymes for DNA replication enter.

Why Folded Membranes Matter

Many important reactions happen on membranes, because the enzymes and transport proteins that carry them out are embedded in the membrane. The more membrane surface area a cell has, the more of these proteins it can hold. Folding a membrane—like the cristae of mitochondria or the stacked thylakoids of chloroplasts—packs a large surface area into a small volume. You will see the same idea again in Topic 2.3 (microvilli) and Unit 3 (electron transport chains).

Misconception Alert

Ribosomes are not part of the endomembrane system. Ribosomes have no membrane. They can be attached to the rough ER, but free and bound ribosomes are identical; where a ribosome works depends on a signal at the start of the protein it is making. Also note that mitochondria and chloroplasts are membrane-bound but are not part of the endomembrane system—they are not connected to it by vesicles, and they arose differently (Topic 2.11).

Key Terms
Endomembrane system
Membranes that make, modify, and transport proteins and lipids, linked directly or by vesicles.
Lumen
The internal space of the ER or another membrane-bound compartment.
Vesicle
Small membrane sac that carries substances between compartments.
Cisternae
Flattened membrane sacs of the ER or Golgi.
Secretion
Release of substances, such as proteins, from a cell by exocytosis.
Turgor pressure
Pressure of the cell contents against the cell wall.
