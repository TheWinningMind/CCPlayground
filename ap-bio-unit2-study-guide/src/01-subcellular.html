TOPIC 2.1

Cell Structure: Subcellular Components

Learning Targets
  • Describe the structure and function of the ribosome, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi complex, mitochondria, lysosomes, vacuoles, and chloroplasts.
  • Explain why ribosomes in every living cell are evidence of common ancestry.
  • Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, and plant and animal cells.
  • Identify organelles in a diagram or micrograph and state their function.

Step 1: What Every Cell Has

The cell theory states that all living things are made of one or more cells, that the cell is the basic unit of life, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells. Despite the enormous diversity of life, every cell—bacterium, amoeba, leaf cell, or neuron—has four things in common:

  1. A plasma membrane — a phospholipid bilayer that separates the inside of the cell from the outside and controls what crosses (Topics 2.4–2.9).
  2. Cytoplasm — everything inside the membrane. The fluid part is the cytosol, a water-based solution where many reactions occur.
  3. DNA — the genetic material, which carries instructions for building proteins.
  4. Ribosomes — the structures that build proteins.

Step 2: Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

Cells are divided into two basic types. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; their DNA sits in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells (protists, fungi, plants, and animals) have a true nucleus surrounded by a double membrane, plus many internal membrane-bound organelles.

Misconception Alert

“Prokaryotes have no organelles” is not quite right. Prokaryotes have no membrane-bound organelles, but they do have ribosomes, which are organelles without a membrane. Some prokaryotes also have internal folds of the plasma membrane where reactions such as photosynthesis take place.

Step 3: Tour the Eukaryotic Cell

The figures below show a typical animal cell and a typical plant cell cut open so you can see inside. Study them before reading the Organelle Gallery, then come back and test yourself by covering the labels.

Misconception Alert

Plant cells have mitochondria too. Chloroplasts capture light energy and make sugar, but plant cells still need mitochondria to break that sugar down and make most of their ATP through cellular respiration. On the exam, “plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria” is always wrong.

Step 4: The 3D Organelle Gallery

Each card shows one structure, the cells it is found in, its structure, and its function. Learn them in pairs: for every structure, say what it does and why its shape suits that job.

Nucleus

Eukaryotes

STRUCTURE Double-membrane nuclear envelope perforated by nuclear pores; contains chromatin (DNA + proteins) and the nucleolus.

FUNCTION Stores and protects DNA; site of transcription. The nucleolus assembles ribosomal subunits. Pores let mRNA out and proteins in.

Ribosome

All cells (no membrane)

STRUCTURE Two subunits (large and small) made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein. Not surrounded by a membrane.

FUNCTION Synthesizes proteins by translating mRNA. Free ribosomes make proteins used in the cytosol; bound ribosomes on rough ER make proteins for membranes, lysosomes, or secretion.

Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum

Eukaryotes

STRUCTURE Network of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae), continuous with the nuclear envelope, studded with ribosomes.

FUNCTION Compartmentalizes the cell; ribosomes make proteins that enter the ER lumen to be folded, modified, and shipped in vesicles to the Golgi.

Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum

Eukaryotes

STRUCTURE Network of membrane tubules with no ribosomes.

FUNCTION Synthesizes lipids (phospholipids, steroids), detoxifies drugs and poisons (especially in liver cells), and stores calcium ions (in muscle cells, it triggers contraction).

Golgi Complex

Eukaryotes

STRUCTURE Stack of flattened membrane sacs with a receiving (cis) face and a shipping (trans) face.

FUNCTION Modifies, sorts, tags, and packages proteins and lipids from the ER (for example, adding sugars to make glycoproteins); sends them out in vesicles.

Mitochondrion

Nearly all eukaryotes (plants too)

STRUCTURE Smooth outer membrane and highly folded inner membrane (folds = cristae) surrounding the fluid matrix; has its own circular DNA and ribosomes.

FUNCTION Site of aerobic cellular respiration; makes most of the cell’s ATP. Cristae increase the surface area for ATP production.

Chloroplast

Plants and algae

STRUCTURE Double membrane surrounding fluid stroma; inside are stacked membrane sacs called thylakoids (a stack = granum); has its own DNA and ribosomes.

FUNCTION Site of photosynthesis. Light reactions occur in thylakoid membranes; the Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma.

Lysosome

Animal cells (mainly)

STRUCTURE Membrane sac containing hydrolytic enzymes that work best at an acidic pH (about 5).

FUNCTION Digests macromolecules, worn-out organelles (autophagy), and engulfed particles or bacteria; can trigger programmed cell death (apoptosis).

Central Vacuole

Plants (large); small vacuoles in other cells

STRUCTURE Large membrane-bound sac (membrane = tonoplast) filled with water, ions, pigments, and waste.

FUNCTION Stores water and solutes; pushes the cytoplasm against the wall to create turgor pressure that keeps the plant firm; may hold toxins that deter herbivores.

Peroxisome

Eukaryotes

STRUCTURE Single-membrane sac containing oxidative enzymes, often with a crystalline core.

FUNCTION Breaks down fatty acids and toxins; produces hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and uses the enzyme catalase to convert it to water and oxygen.

Cytoskeleton

Eukaryotes (similar proteins in prokaryotes)

STRUCTURE Network of protein fibers: hollow microtubules (tubulin), thin microfilaments (actin), and rope-like intermediate filaments.

FUNCTION Supports cell shape; anchors organelles; tracks for vesicle movement; moves chromosomes during division; forms cilia and flagella (microtubules); muscle contraction (actin).

Centrosome and Centrioles

Animal cells

STRUCTURE A region near the nucleus containing a pair of centrioles at right angles, each built of nine triplets of microtubules.

FUNCTION Organizes microtubules; helps form the spindle that separates chromosomes during cell division.

Plasma Membrane

All cells

STRUCTURE Phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins, cholesterol (animals), and carbohydrate chains (fluid mosaic model).

FUNCTION Selectively permeable boundary; controls transport; receives signals; cell recognition (Topics 2.4–2.9).

Cell Wall

Plants, fungi, most prokaryotes

STRUCTURE Rigid layer outside the plasma membrane: cellulose fibers (plants), chitin (fungi), or peptidoglycan (bacteria).

FUNCTION Provides shape and support; protects the cell; resists bursting when water rushes in. It is porous, so it does not control what enters.

Exam Tip

Ribosomes are the universal organelle. Every living cell has ribosomes made of rRNA and protein, and the genetic code they read is nearly universal. When a question asks for evidence that all organisms share a common ancestor, ribosomes (along with DNA as genetic material and the plasma membrane) are a strong answer.

Key Terms
Prokaryotic cell
A cell without a nucleus or membrane-bound organelles (bacteria and archaea).
Eukaryotic cell
A cell with a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
Nucleoid
Region of a prokaryotic cell where its DNA is located.
Cytosol
The fluid portion of the cytoplasm.
Organelle
A specialized structure that performs a specific job within a cell.
Ribosome
rRNA–protein complex that synthesizes proteins.
Endomembrane system
The nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, and plasma membrane.
Cristae
Folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Thylakoid
Membrane sac inside a chloroplast where light reactions occur.
Stroma
Fluid inside the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
Matrix
Fluid inside the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Hydrolytic enzyme
An enzyme that breaks bonds by hydrolysis.
