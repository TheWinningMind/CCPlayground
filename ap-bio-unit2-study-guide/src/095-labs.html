Unit 2 Lab Investigations

Unit 2 is tested heavily through experiments: free-response questions often describe a diffusion or osmosis lab and ask you to identify variables, graph data, calculate values, and explain results. This chapter walks you through the three classic Unit 2 investigations step by step. Each lab includes sample class data so you can practice the analysis even if you have not done the lab yourself. Answers to the analysis questions are in Part 4.

Safety First

Wear goggles when working with vinegar, iodine, and other chemicals. Iodine (Lugol’s solution) stains skin and clothing. Use cutting tools carefully and cut away from your body. Never taste anything in the lab. Follow your teacher’s instructions for cleanup and disposal.

Lab 1: Cell Size and Diffusion

Question: How does the size of a cell affect how efficiently substances can diffuse into it?

Background: Agar is a gel that can model a cell. Agar made with the pH indicator phenolphthalein is pink in basic conditions and turns colorless when acid (vinegar) diffuses in. By measuring how far the color change reaches, you can see how much of each “cell” the acid has reached in a fixed time.

Materials: phenolphthalein agar block, plastic knife, metric ruler, 250 mL beaker, 150 mL white vinegar, spoon, paper towels, timer.

Procedure

  1. Cut three cubes from the agar: 1 cm, 2 cm, and 3 cm on each side. Cut carefully so the faces are flat.
  2. Calculate the surface area, volume, and SA:V ratio of each cube and record them in the data table.
  3. Place all three cubes in the beaker and cover them completely with vinegar. Start the timer.
  4. After 10 minutes, remove the cubes with the spoon and blot them dry.
  5. Cut each cube in half. Measure the depth (in cm) that the color change has penetrated from the surface.
  6. Calculate the volume of each cube that has not changed color (a smaller cube in the center), then the percentage of the volume that the acid has reached.

Analysis Questions

  1. Identify the independent and dependent variables in this investigation.
  2. The depth of diffusion was the same for all three cubes. Explain why the percentage of volume diffused was not the same.
  3. Describe the relationship between SA:V ratio and the percentage of volume diffused.
  4. Use the results to explain why most cells are microscopic.
  5. A student cuts a fourth agar piece shaped like a flat sheet, 3 cm × 3 cm × 0.5 cm. Predict whether its % volume diffused would be higher or lower than the 1 cm cube’s, and justify your prediction.

Lab 2: Diffusion and Osmosis with Dialysis Tubing

Question: Which molecules can pass through a selectively permeable membrane, and which way does water move?

Background: Dialysis tubing is an artificial selectively permeable membrane with tiny pores. Small molecules such as water, glucose, and iodine pass through; large molecules such as starch do not. Iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch. Glucose test strips change color when glucose is present.

Materials: 20 cm of soaked dialysis tubing, two clips or string, 15% glucose/1% starch solution, distilled water, Lugol’s iodine, glucose test strips, 400 mL beaker, balance.

Procedure

  1. Tie or clip one end of the tubing. Fill it with 15 mL of the glucose/starch solution and clip the other end, leaving a little room.
  2. Rinse and blot the bag; record its initial mass and color. Test the solution inside for glucose and record the result.
  3. Fill the beaker with distilled water and add iodine until the water is amber. Test the beaker water for glucose and record the result.
  4. Submerge the bag in the beaker for 30 minutes.
  5. Remove the bag, blot it, and record its final mass and color. Record the beaker’s color, and test the beaker water for glucose again.

Analysis Questions

  1. Which substances moved into the bag and which moved out? Justify each answer using the data.
  2. Explain why starch did not leave the bag.
  3. Calculate the percent change in mass of the bag, and explain the change in terms of osmosis.
  4. The bag was initially hypertonic to the beaker. Explain what this means and how it relates to your answer for question 8.
  5. If the experiment were repeated with the starch/glucose solution in the beaker and distilled water with iodine in the bag, predict the final colors of the bag and beaker and the direction of mass change.

Lab 3: Water Potential of Plant Tissue

Question: What is the water potential of carrot cells?

Background: When plant tissue is placed in a solution, water moves from higher to lower water potential. The tissue gains mass in a solution with a higher water potential and loses mass in one with a lower water potential. At the concentration where the mass does not change, the solution is isotonic to the cells. Using that concentration, you can calculate the solute potential of the cells.

Materials: carrots, cork borer, ruler, scalpel, balance, six cups, sucrose solutions (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 M), plastic wrap, paper towels.

Procedure

  1. Use the cork borer to cut 24 carrot cylinders of the same length (about 3 cm). Remove any skin.
  2. Label six cups with the sucrose molarities and add 100 mL of the matching solution to each.
  3. Blot four cylinders, measure their combined mass, and place them in a cup. Repeat for each cup.
  4. Cover the cups with plastic wrap to limit evaporation and leave them overnight (about 24 hours) at room temperature (22°C).
  5. Remove the cylinders, blot them gently in the same way, and measure the final combined mass for each cup.
  6. Calculate the percent change in mass for each cup, then graph % change (y) against molarity (x) and draw a line of best fit.

Analysis Questions

  1. Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable, and two controlled variables.
  2. Explain why the cylinders were weighed in groups and why percent change was calculated.
  3. Use the data to estimate the molarity of sucrose that is isotonic to the carrot cells.
  4. Calculate the solute potential of the carrot cells at 22°C, assuming their solute concentration equals your answer to question 13.
  5. The cups were covered with plastic wrap. Predict how the results would change if the cups were left uncovered for several days, and justify your prediction.
Exam Tip: Lab Questions

Free-response lab questions reward precise vocabulary. Name the independent variable (what you changed) and the dependent variable (what you measured) with units. For controlled variables, give specific ones (tissue type, cylinder size, temperature, time) rather than “everything else.” When you explain a result, always connect it to water potential or concentration gradients.
