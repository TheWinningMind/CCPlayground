TOPIC 2.9

Mechanisms of Transport

Learning Targets
  • Describe, step by step, how the sodium–potassium pump uses ATP to move ions against their gradients.
  • Explain how the membrane potential and electrochemical gradients form.
  • Explain how cotransport uses an existing gradient to move a second substance.
  • Compare phagocytosis, pinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis.

Step 1: The Sodium–Potassium Pump

The sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase) is the best-known example of primary active transport—active transport powered directly by ATP. In animal cells it keeps Na+ concentration high outside the cell and K+ concentration high inside. Each cycle moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, both against their concentration gradients.

  1. Na+ binds. The pump is open to the cytoplasm. Three Na+ ions from inside the cell bind to it.
  2. ATP is used. Binding of Na+ triggers the pump to hydrolyze ATP. A phosphate group is attached to the pump (phosphorylation), and ADP is released.
  3. Shape change; Na+ released outside. Phosphorylation changes the pump’s shape so it opens to the outside. Its affinity for Na+ drops, and the three Na+ ions are released outside the cell.
  4. K+ binds; phosphate leaves; K+ released inside. Two K+ ions from outside bind. This causes the phosphate to be released, the pump returns to its original shape, and the K+ ions are released into the cytoplasm. The cycle repeats.

Step 2: Membrane Potential and Electrochemical Gradients

Because the pump moves three positive charges out for every two it brings in, and because K+ leaks out through channels, the inside of a typical animal cell is negative relative to the outside—about −70 mV in a resting neuron. This voltage across the membrane is the membrane potential. An ion is therefore pushed by two forces: its concentration gradient and the electrical gradient. Together these make up the ion’s electrochemical gradient. The membrane potential is the basis of nerve impulses (Unit 4).

Step 3: Cotransport (Secondary Active Transport)

A gradient built by a pump is stored energy. In cotransport, a carrier protein lets one substance flow down its gradient and uses that energy to drag a second substance up its gradient. No ATP is used by the cotransporter itself, but ATP was spent earlier by the pump that built the gradient, so this is called secondary active transport.

  • Intestinal cells: the sodium–glucose cotransporter lets Na+ flow into the cell (down its gradient, maintained by the Na+/K+ pump) and brings glucose in with it, even when glucose is more concentrated inside.
  • Plant cells: a proton pump uses ATP to push H+ out of the cell. The H+ gradient then powers the H+/sucrose cotransporter, which loads sucrose into cells for transport through the phloem.

Exam Tip

If a question says a transport process stops when ATP production is blocked (for example, with a poison such as cyanide), the process is active transport or bulk transport. If transport continues without ATP but still needs a protein, it is facilitated diffusion. Cotransport stops eventually, once the pump-maintained gradient runs down.

Misconception Alert

The sodium–potassium pump is not a channel. Ions never flow freely through it. It is a carrier protein that changes shape, and it always moves Na+ out and K+ in, against their gradients. Ions flowing down their gradients through open channels is facilitated diffusion.

Key Terms
Sodium–potassium pump
Carrier that uses ATP to move 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in.
Primary active transport
Active transport powered directly by ATP.
Membrane potential
Voltage across a membrane due to unequal distribution of ions.
Electrochemical gradient
Combined concentration and electrical gradient for an ion.
Proton pump
Active transporter that pumps H+ across a membrane.
Cotransport
Coupled movement of a substance up its gradient with another moving down its gradient.
Phagocytosis
Endocytosis of large particles (“cell eating”).
Pinocytosis
Nonspecific endocytosis of fluid (“cell drinking”).
Receptor-mediated endocytosis
Endocytosis of specific molecules bound to receptors.
