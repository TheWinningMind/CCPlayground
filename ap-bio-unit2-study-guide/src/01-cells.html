TOPIC 2.1

Cell Structure: Subcellular Components

Learning Targets
  • Describe the structure and function of ribosomes, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi complex, mitochondria, lysosomes, vacuoles, and chloroplasts.
  • Compare the structures found in prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Explain why the presence of ribosomes in every cell is evidence of common ancestry.
  • Identify the structures that distinguish plant cells from animal cells.

The Cell Theory

The cell theory states that (1) all living things are made of one or more cells, (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things, and (3) all cells come from pre-existing cells. Every cell, from a bacterium to a human neuron, shares four basic features: a plasma membrane that separates the inside from the outside, cytoplasm (the fluid interior and everything suspended in it), DNA as its genetic material, and ribosomes that make proteins.

Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Cells come in two basic designs. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Their DNA is usually a single circular chromosome located in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, and many also carry small circles of DNA called plasmids. Eukaryotic cells (protists, fungi, plants, and animals) have a true nucleus enclosed by a membrane and many internal membrane-bound compartments called organelles.

Misconception Alert

“Prokaryotes have no organelles” is not quite right. Prokaryotes have no membrane-bound organelles, but they do have ribosomes, which are sometimes called non-membranous organelles. Also, prokaryotes are not simply “primitive.” They are the most abundant and metabolically diverse organisms on Earth.

A Tour of the Eukaryotic Cell

The Nucleus

The nucleus holds most of the cell’s DNA, organized with proteins into chromatin. It is surrounded by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane perforated by nuclear pores that control which molecules (such as mRNA and proteins) enter and leave. Inside, the nucleolus is the region where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is made and ribosome subunits are assembled.

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are made of ribosomal RNA and protein. They are not surrounded by a membrane. Their job is to synthesize proteins by reading the instructions in messenger RNA. Ribosomes are found in two places in eukaryotic cells:

  • Free ribosomes, suspended in the cytosol, make proteins that will function in the cytosol (for example, enzymes of glycolysis).
  • Bound ribosomes, attached to the rough ER, make proteins that will be inserted into membranes, delivered to certain organelles such as lysosomes, or secreted from the cell.

Every living cell—bacterial, archaeal, and eukaryotic—has ribosomes, and their structure and the genetic code they read are remarkably similar across all three domains. This is strong evidence that all living organisms share a common ancestor.

The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membrane-enclosed tubes and flattened sacs that is continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It comes in two forms:

  • Rough ER is studded with ribosomes. It makes proteins destined for secretion, membranes, or lysosomes, and it compartmentalizes them as they are made. Rough ER also begins modifying proteins, for example by adding carbohydrate chains.
  • Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It synthesizes lipids (including phospholipids and steroids), detoxifies drugs and poisons (especially in liver cells), and stores calcium ions (especially in muscle cells).

The Golgi Complex

The Golgi complex (also called the Golgi apparatus or Golgi body) is a stack of flattened membrane sacs called cisternae. It receives proteins in vesicles from the ER, ensures their correct folding, chemically modifies them (for example, by adding or trimming carbohydrates to make glycoproteins), and then sorts and packages them into vesicles addressed to their final destination. Think of it as the cell’s shipping center.

Mitochondria

Mitochondria are the sites of aerobic cellular respiration, where energy from food molecules is transferred to ATP. Each mitochondrion has a double membrane: a smooth outer membrane and a highly folded inner membrane. The folds are called cristae, and the fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix. Mitochondria also have their own small circular DNA and their own ribosomes—clues to their origin that you will study in Topic 2.11.

Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-enclosed sacs containing hydrolytic enzymes—enzymes that break down macromolecules by hydrolysis. The inside of a lysosome is acidic (about pH 5), which is the pH at which these enzymes work best. Lysosomes are common in animal cells; plant cells use their vacuoles for many of the same jobs.

Vacuoles

Vacuoles are membrane-bound sacs with many functions, including storage of water, ions, and macromolecules and the release of waste. Plant cells usually have a single large central vacuole surrounded by a membrane called the tonoplast. Freshwater protists may have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water (see Topic 2.8).

Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts, found in plants and algae, are the sites of photosynthesis. Like mitochondria, they have a double outer membrane, their own circular DNA, and their own ribosomes. Inside is a third membrane system: flattened sacs called thylakoids, which are stacked into grana (singular: granum) and surrounded by fluid called the stroma.

Other Structures You Should Know

  • Cytoskeleton: a network of protein fibers (microtubules, intermediate filaments, and actin microfilaments) that supports the cell’s shape, anchors organelles, and moves vesicles and chromosomes.
  • Centrosome: in animal cells, the region near the nucleus where microtubules are organized; it contains a pair of centrioles.
  • Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the plasma membrane of plants (made of cellulose), fungi (chitin), and most bacteria (peptidoglycan). It protects the cell and keeps it from bursting.
  • Peroxisomes: small membrane-bound organelles that break down fatty acids and convert toxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to water.
Exam Tip

The AP Course and Exam Description focuses on seven subcellular components: ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi complex, mitochondria, lysosomes, vacuoles, and chloroplasts. For each one, be ready to state (1) its structure, (2) its function, and (3) whether it is found in prokaryotes, plants, animals, or all cells. Questions frequently ask you to identify which organelle would be most abundant in a cell with a particular job.

TOPIC 2.2

Cell Structure and Function

Learning Targets
  • Explain how the structure of each organelle supports its function.
  • Trace the path of a secreted protein through the endomembrane system.
  • Explain how the folded inner membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts increase their efficiency.
  • Predict how a defect in an organelle would affect the cell.

The Endomembrane System

Many of the membranes in a eukaryotic cell work together as a single system. The endomembrane system includes the nuclear envelope, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi complex, lysosomes, vesicles, vacuoles, and the plasma membrane. These membranes are connected either directly or by vesicles—small membrane bubbles that bud off one membrane and fuse with another. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not part of the endomembrane system.

Follow a digestive enzyme made by a pancreatic cell from start to finish:

  1. A ribosome begins translating the mRNA in the cytosol. A short signal at the start of the protein directs the ribosome to the rough ER.
  2. The growing protein is threaded into the ER interior (the lumen), where it folds and may have carbohydrates added.
  3. The protein leaves the ER in a transport vesicle and travels to the cis face (receiving side) of the Golgi.
  4. The Golgi modifies the protein further and sorts it. It leaves the trans face (shipping side) in a new vesicle.
  5. The vesicle moves to the plasma membrane along the cytoskeleton and fuses with it, releasing the protein outside the cell by exocytosis.
Misconception Alert

The Golgi does not make proteins. Ribosomes make proteins. The Golgi modifies, sorts, and packages proteins that have already been made. Similarly, the smooth ER does not make proteins; it makes lipids.

Structure Fits Function: Organelle by Organelle

Endoplasmic Reticulum

The ER’s enormous membrane surface provides mechanical support and a highway for intracellular transport. Because the rough ER encloses newly made proteins in its lumen, it keeps them compartmentalized and separate from the cytosol. Cells that secrete large amounts of protein, such as pancreatic cells that make digestive enzymes and plasma cells that make antibodies, are packed with rough ER. Cells that make steroid hormones (in the adrenal glands and gonads) and liver cells that detoxify drugs are rich in smooth ER.

Golgi Complex

The Golgi has a direction. Vesicles from the ER fuse with the cis face, and proteins are modified step by step as they move through the cisternae toward the trans face. Each cisterna contains different enzymes, so the Golgi works like an assembly line. Molecular “tags” added in the Golgi act like shipping labels that direct each vesicle to its target: the plasma membrane, a lysosome, or the outside of the cell.

Mitochondria

The double membrane creates separate compartments where different metabolic reactions can happen. The Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle) occurs in the matrix. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are embedded in the inner membrane. The cristae fold the inner membrane back and forth, greatly increasing its surface area, so more electron transport chains and ATP synthase enzymes can fit, and more ATP can be made. Muscle cells and sperm cells, which use large amounts of energy, contain many mitochondria.

Chloroplasts

The thylakoid membranes contain chlorophyll and the electron transport proteins of the light-dependent reactions. Stacking thylakoids into grana packs a huge membrane surface area into a small space, so more light can be captured. The Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions), which uses CO2 to build sugar, takes place in the stroma. Leaf cells in the sunniest part of a leaf contain the most chloroplasts.

Lysosomes

Lysosomes carry out intracellular digestion. They fuse with food vacuoles formed by phagocytosis and digest the contents. They also recycle the cell’s own worn-out organelles and macromolecules, a process called autophagy, and they play a role in apoptosis, the programmed death of cells (for example, the cells between the fingers of a developing human embryo). The lysosome membrane protects the rest of the cell from its own digestive enzymes, and because the enzymes work best at pH 5, any that leak into the neutral cytosol (pH about 7.2) are much less active.

Vacuoles

Vacuoles store and release macromolecules and waste. In plants, the large central vacuole takes up water, and the water pushes the cytoplasm against the cell wall, creating turgor pressure that keeps nonwoody plant parts firm. When a plant loses water, its central vacuoles shrink, turgor pressure falls, and the plant wilts. The central vacuole also stores pigments, ions, and toxic compounds that discourage animals from eating the plant.

Exam Tip

When a question describes a cell with a special job, think about what it needs most. Lots of protein for export? Rough ER and Golgi. Lots of lipid or detoxification? Smooth ER. Lots of energy? Mitochondria. Lots of digestion? Lysosomes. Then explain the connection with “because.”
