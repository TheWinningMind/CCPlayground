Question 1 | A | 2.4.A.1

Answer: Its nonpolar fatty-acid interior

Explanation: The hydrophobic core imposes an energetic barrier to charged and many polar substances; the other features alone do not explain selectivity.

Question 2 | D | 2.4.A.2

Answer: Oxygen

Explanation: Small nonpolar O₂ can diffuse through the lipid core without membrane proteins.

Question 3 | C | 2.4.A.3

Answer: The bilayer core is energetically unfavorable for a charged particle

Explanation: The ion and its hydration shell interact poorly with the nonpolar interior; size alone is not the main reason.

Common pitfall: Do not treat molecular size as the sole determinant; charge is critically important.

Question 4 | B | 2.4.A.2

Answer: CO₂ and N₂

Explanation: Both CO₂ and N₂ are small, nonpolar molecules. Water is polar; glucose is relatively large and polar; sodium is charged.

Question 5 | B | 2.4.A.3

Answer: Water

Explanation: Water is small and uncharged, so some molecules diffuse directly; transport proteins can markedly increase overall movement.

Question 6 | A | 2.4.A.1

Answer: aqueous environments on both sides of the bilayer

Explanation: Hydrophilic heads contact the aqueous external and cytosolic environments.

Question 7 | D | 2.4.B.1

Answer: The plasma membrane usually lies inside the wall and controls selective exchange

Explanation: A cell with a wall still has a plasma membrane; the wall is usually exterior to it.

Question 8 | C | 2.4.A.2

Answer: CO₂ is a small nonpolar molecule overall

Explanation: Molecular polarity, not the presence of oxygen atoms, predicts whether CO₂ crosses the lipid interior readily.

Common pitfall: A molecule with polar bonds can still be nonpolar overall, as in CO₂.

Question 9 | C | 2.4.B.1

Answer: providing mechanical resistance against excessive expansion

Explanation: Wall rigidity opposes expansion and helps prevent osmotic lysis; it does not prevent all water movement.

Common pitfall: The protective wall does not make the plasma membrane impermeable to water.

Question 10 | B | 2.4.B.1

Answer: A typical bacterial cell

Explanation: Bacteria generally have cell walls; the animal cell types listed normally do not.

Question 11 | A | 2.4.A.3

Answer: Lower, because the lipid interior is nonpolar

Explanation: Even when size is comparable, charge strongly reduces passage through a hydrophobic bilayer.

Common pitfall: A similarly sized ion is not comparable to a nonpolar molecule in lipid solubility.

Question 12 | D | 2.4.A.2

Answer: Glucose is large and polar relative to freely permeating gases

Explanation: Glucose contains several polar hydroxyl groups and is relatively large; its low permeability does not imply charge or chemical conversion.

Question 13 | D | 2.4.A.2–3

Answer: Oxygen crosses readily, whereas water crosses in smaller amounts

Explanation: O₂ is small and nonpolar; some H₂O molecules also cross directly, but less readily.

Common pitfall: Water crosses directly in limited amounts; it is not absolutely excluded.

Question 14 | C | 2.4.A.1

Explanation: Region B is the hydrocarbon interior composed mainly of fatty-acid tails.

Question 15 | B | 2.4.A.2

Explanation: A compatible channel provides an aqueous route across the otherwise unfavorable hydrophobic core.

Question 16 | A | 2.4.B.1

Explanation: Living cells require a membrane boundary even when they also possess a wall.

Question 17 | A | 2.4.B.1

Answer: They can serve as structural boundaries and barriers to some substances

Explanation: Walls differ in composition but can provide structural support and restrict some movement, limiting osmotic lysis.

Question 18 | D | 2.4.A.2

Answer: Rapid passage of O₂

Explanation: Small nonpolar O₂ diffuses directly, whereas high flux of ions or proteins would require alternative explanations.

Question 19 | C | 2.4.A.1

Answer: Selective permeability

Explanation: Selective permeability describes different crossing probabilities for different substances.

Question 20 | B | 2.4.B.1

Answer: Percentage of lysed cells with intact versus experimentally weakened walls

Explanation: Comparing lysis frequency under matched conditions isolates the predicted effect of wall integrity.

Question 21 | B | 2.4.A.2

Answer: Small nonpolar molecules traverse the bilayer faster than the listed polar or charged substances

Explanation: The high O₂ and CO₂ values, compared with glucose and Na⁺, support selective permeability based on molecular properties.

Question 22 | A | 2.4.A.2–3

Answer: 7.5

Explanation: 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5. The model rate comparison does not establish a universal permeability ratio.

Common pitfall: A fold change is a ratio. The correct calculation is 90 ÷ 12.

Question 23 | D | 2.4.A.3

Explanation: Ionic charge and hydration strongly discourage passage through the nonpolar interior.

Question 24 | C | 2.4.A.2

Question 25 | C | 2.4.A.1–3

Question 26 | B | 2.4.B.1

Question 30 | C | 2.4.B.1

Question 34 | D | 2.4.A.2

Question 35 | C | 2.4.A.2

Question 36 | B | 2.4.B.1 / Skill 5.D

Answer: Reject the null, because p = 0.004 is below 0.05

Question 37 | B | 2.4.B.1 / Skill 5.D

Answer: There is insufficient evidence here to reject the null of equal means

Explanation: Failing to reject equality does not prove the two means are identical or biologically equivalent.

Common pitfall: Failure to reject H₀ is not proof that two groups are equivalent.

Question 40 | C | 2.4.B.1 / Skill 5.D

Answer: Repeated independent trials showing indistinguishable mean lysis in wall-intact and wall-weakened groups under dilute conditions

Explanation: If weakening walls does not change lysis under sufficiently powered controlled tests, the predicted protective effect would lack support.
