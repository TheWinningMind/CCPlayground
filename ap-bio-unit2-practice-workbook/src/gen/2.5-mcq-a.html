Fully explained solutions

1. A — The membrane limits movement of X, allowing different concentrations on its two sides.

Selective permeability can limit solute exchange, permitting unequal concentrations. A gradient does not require synthesis of solute or a single obligatory direction of motion.

EK 2.5.A.1

2. B — Net movement of the solute from outside to inside without direct metabolic energy input.

Passive transport proceeds down the concentration gradient without direct metabolic energy. Individual molecules move at all times, even when the net flux becomes zero.

EK 2.5.A.2

3. B — Active transport requires a direct energy input for the transport process.

Direct energy input is the defining distinction here. Both mechanisms can affect gradients, and passive movement can use transport proteins (developed in Topic 2.6).

EK 2.5.A.3

4. D — Energy-dependent active transport of the solute into the cell.

Net entry from 8 mM to 36 mM is against the concentration gradient and is consistent with active transport. The observation alone should not specify a particular pump.

EK 2.5.A.3

5. A — Individual molecules cross in both directions, but net transport is zero.

Equal exchange in opposite directions yields zero net flux, not zero molecular motion. Dynamic equilibrium is compatible with continual random movement.

EK 2.5.A.2

6. D — From chamber A to chamber B.

Initially the greater concentration of R in A favors net diffusion toward B; passive movement does not require ATP. The resulting gradient decreases over time in a closed system.

EK 2.5.A.2

7. A — The plasma membrane bends inward around extracellular material.

Endocytosis begins with membrane invagination and packaging of extracellular material into a vesicle. Vesicle fusion with the surface is characteristic of exocytosis.

EK 2.5.B.1

8. B — An internal vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane.

Membrane fusion connects the vesicle lumen with the extracellular environment. Inward folding and vesicle formation describe endocytosis.

EK 2.5.B.1

9. C — Both involve coordinated vesicle formation, movement, or membrane fusion supported by cellular energy.

Vesicular transport is energy-dependent and moves large cargo or large amounts of cargo. Its energetic requirement is not determined solely by the cargo concentration gradient.

EK 2.5.B.1

10. C — Inside a membrane-bounded vesicle within the cytoplasm.

Engulfed material becomes enclosed by a membrane-derived internal vesicle. The process does not automatically target the nucleus.

EK 2.5.B.1

11. B — From the cell interior to the external solution.

Passive net transport proceeds from higher to lower concentration across a permeable boundary. A higher internal concentration initially favors net export.

EK 2.5.A.2

12. D — The transport rate decreases when the process is specifically deprived of its required energy source.

Energy dependence strengthens an active-transport explanation, especially when viability and other variables are controlled. An uphill concentration change alone is suggestive but may require testing to eliminate alternatives.

EK 2.5.A.3

13. D — The difference between the amounts moving in opposite directions per unit time.

Net flux compares opposing movements. Molecules can move both ways even while net movement is down a gradient.

EK 2.5.A.2

14. B — A membrane-bound secretory vesicle releases stored protein into the external medium.

Exocytosis releases cargo by vesicle fusion at the plasma membrane. The other processes involve inward uptake from outside the cell.

EK 2.5.B.1

15. B — A concentration difference for B may persist longer than a comparable difference for A.

Different permeabilities cause different rates of equilibration; a restricted solute can retain a gradient. Restricted passive movement does not prove that the solute has an active transporter.

EK 2.5.A.1

16. B — P represents endocytosis; Q represents exocytosis.

Internal formation from the plasma membrane is endocytosis; fusion of an internal vesicle with the surface is exocytosis. Both are energy-requiring bulk transport processes.

EK 2.5.B.1

17. D — The measured concentration difference across the membrane becomes smaller over time.

Gradient magnitude is characterized by the concentration difference between the compartments. A smaller difference directly indicates a weaker gradient under a simple concentration model.

EK 2.5.A.1

18. D — The vesicle lumen becomes continuous with the extracellular space and cargo is released.

Vesicle fusion opens a path between the lumen and the outside while maintaining the membrane boundary. It does not require proteins to pass through the membrane core.

EK 2.5.B.1

19. A — A concentration gradient and a pathway through which the solute can move.

For a permeant solute, the concentration difference drives net diffusion without direct metabolic energy. Vesicle-based movement is a different mechanism.

EK 2.5.A.2

20. C — A cell engulfs intact extracellular particles into intracellular vesicles.

Endocytosis moves whole particles or large packages of extracellular material into vesicles. This does not require each cargo molecule to traverse the lipid bilayer individually.

EK 2.5.B.1

21. C — Quench surface fluorescence with a membrane-impermeant reagent before measuring internal signal.

A reagent that quenches only external fluorescence helps separate internalized particles from surface-bound ones. Total cell-associated signal alone can overestimate uptake.

EK 2.5.B.1

22. D — The inhibitor also causes extensive membrane rupture and cell death during the assay.

Widespread injury could reduce uptake by many nonspecific mechanisms, undermining an energy-specific conclusion. Viability and membrane integrity controls are essential.

EK 2.5.A.3

23. D — Net entry is against the stated concentration gradient, so an energy-dependent process is a testable explanation.

Entry against a known concentration gradient is consistent with active transport, but identifying the molecular mechanism requires further experiments. Inward direction alone does not establish passivity.

EK 2.5.A.3

24. C — Microscopy shows intracellular cargo vesicles fusing with intact plasma membranes during release.

Direct visualization of vesicle fusion with intact plasma membranes is diagnostic supporting evidence for exocytosis. Fluorescence outside cells alone could reflect membrane damage.

EK 2.5.B.1

25. A — Loss of gradient maintenance can alter the direction or magnitude of subsequent passive net movement.

Active transport can establish or maintain a gradient, which can later drive passive movement. Passive movement does not itself begin consuming ATP when a separate active process stops.

EK 2.5.A.1

26. A — From chamber A to chamber B.

Initially A has higher tracer concentration than B, so net passive movement favors B. ATP is not directly required in the specified permeable membrane model.

EK 2.5.A.2 | stimulus A

27. B — 30 concentration units.

At 5 minutes, A − B = 65 − 35 = 30 concentration units. A concentration difference is not the same as either absolute concentration.

EK 2.5.A.1 | stimulus A

28. D — The concentrations are equal, so the expected net movement of X is zero.

Equal concentrations across an equally accessible membrane mean no net diffusion, even though individual molecules continue moving. The data do not demonstrate a sudden permeability change.

EK 2.5.A.2 | stimulus A

29. D — 6%.

At 10 minutes, the difference is 53 − 47 = 6, compared with an initial difference of 100. Thus 6/100 × 100 = 6%.

EK 2.5.A.1 | stimulus A

30. B — With matched conditions, the concentration difference decreases at a similar rate despite the inhibitor, while membrane integrity is preserved.

Persistence of comparable flux without direct energy input supports passive transport. Matching conditions and maintaining membrane integrity prevent confounding explanations.

EK 2.5.A.2 | stimulus A

31. C — Internal Y increases while already exceeding the external Y concentration.

The cells increase internal Y from 35 to 45 mM while external Y is held at 5 mM. This is net inward movement against the stated concentration gradient.

EK 2.5.A.3 | stimulus B

32. C — An increase of 10 mM.

The difference is final minus initial: 45 − 35 = 10 mM. The units are concentration, not absolute amount transported.

EK 2.5.A.3 | stimulus B

33. A — Untreated versus ATP-restricted cells, with concentration and viability measured under matched conditions.

The matched comparison isolates the energy intervention while maintaining comparable cell condition. Unmatched tissues or concentrations introduce confounding variables.

EK 2.5.A.3 | stimulus B

34. C — Restoring energy nearly restores uptake, supporting a reversible energy-dependent contribution.

Rescue toward the untreated response supports an energy-dependent component and limits a purely irreversible-damage explanation. It does not identify a particular transporter.

EK 2.5.A.3 | stimulus B

35. B — Measure permeability to a membrane-impermeant viability dye in each treatment.

A membrane-impermeant dye can reveal compromised membranes. A matched integrity measurement helps distinguish a specific energy-related uptake effect from nonspecific cell injury.

EK 2.5.A.3 | stimulus B

36. A — Endocytosis of beads at the plasma membrane.

Particles are engulfed by the plasma membrane into intracellular vesicles in endocytosis. Intact beads do not ordinarily cross a bilayer as individual dissolved solutes.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus C

37. A — 7 vesicles per cell.

The change is 9 − 2 = 7 vesicles per cell. This is an observed change in the mean, not a count for one individual cell.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus C

38. D — 10 vesicles per cell.

The control mean is 14 and the inhibited mean is 4, giving 14 − 4 = 10 additional vesicles per cell.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus C

39. B — It reduces the chance that externally attached beads will be counted as internalized.

The quencher suppresses external fluorescence and improves measurement specificity for internalized beads. It does not drive uptake or modify beads into transportable solutes.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus C

40. A — Repeat the assay with an independent intracellular uptake measurement and a treatment washout or rescue condition.

Orthogonal uptake evidence and a rescue design test whether the phenotype reflects endocytosis rather than fluorescence artifacts. Matched initial binding is also important.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus C

41. C — Exocytosis through fusion of internal vesicles with the plasma membrane.

Exocytosis releases a vesicle lumen to the outside following membrane fusion. Preloading helps distinguish release from new cargo synthesis.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus D

42. A — 29 arbitrary units.

At 10 minutes the means differ by 37 − 8 = 29 arbitrary units. This is a difference in accumulated extracellular cargo.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus D

43. A — Similar, low rupture rates make extensive cell lysis an unlikely explanation for the treatment difference.

The near-1% lysis rates reduce concern that release differences are explained by extensive membrane rupture. They do not on their own identify the precise fusion protein.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus D

44. C — Time-lapse microscopy showing cargo-bearing vesicles merging with the plasma membrane before cargo release.

Direct vesicle-to-membrane fusion imaging links cargo release to the proposed mechanism. Bulk fluorescence alone measures cargo accumulation but not the fusion step.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus D

45. C — Accumulation of cargo-containing vesicles inside the inhibited cells.

If packaging continues while release is blocked, intracellular cargo-bearing vesicles should accumulate. This prediction distinguishes fusion failure from cargo-production failure.

EK 2.5.B.1 | stimulus D

Teacher/editor notes

Common judgment error: an energy-restriction result is not proof of a named protein or exclusive mechanism. Separate observed changes, mechanistic inferences, and the controls required to test the inference.
