Learning objective(s): 2.7.A–2.7.B • 25 retrieval prompts • Answers with explanations

01. Osmosis — Water moves overall from higher to lower water potential.

02. Hypotonic solution — A hypotonic external solution favors net water entry.

03. Hypertonic solution — A hypertonic external solution favors net water loss.

04. Isotonic solution — Isotonic conditions produce no sustained net swelling or shrinking.

05. Tonicity — Tonicity concerns effective osmotic effects on the cell, not merely total solute content.

06. Water potential (Ψ) — Water flows from higher Ψ to lower Ψ.

07. Solute potential (Ψs) — For a solution, solute potential becomes more negative with increasing solute concentration.

08. Pressure potential (Ψp) — Positive pressure potential contributes to water potential in many turgid plant cells.

09. Ψ = Ψp + Ψs — Total water potential is the sum of pressure and solute potentials.

10. Ψs = −iCRT — Solute potential is calculated using the ionization factor, concentration, pressure constant, and absolute temperature.

11. Osmolarity — Osmolarity counts dissolved particles that contribute to osmotic effects.

12. Osmoregulation — Osmoregulation maintains internal water and solute conditions.

13. Contractile vacuole — Contractile vacuoles help freshwater protists avoid excessive water accumulation.

14. Central vacuole — The central vacuole helps maintain plant-cell turgor.

15. Turgor pressure — Turgor pressure contributes to stiffness and support in living plant tissues.

16. Turgid — Turgid plant cells have positive pressure against the wall.

17. Flaccid — A flaccid cell has low turgor pressure.

18. Plasmolysis — Plasmolysis typically occurs when a plant cell loses water in a hypertonic environment.

19. Crenation — Crenation is often illustrated by red blood cells in a hypertonic solution.

20. Hemolysis — Hypotonic conditions can cause red blood cells to swell and burst.

21. Estimated isotonic concentration — At the isotonic point, net water gain and loss are approximately balanced.

22. Percentage change in mass — The sign indicates net gain or loss of mass.

23. Ionization factor (i) — For ideal nonelectrolytes such as sucrose, i is approximately 1.

24. Pressure constant (R) — Using this value yields solute potential in bars when concentration is in mol/L and temperature in kelvin.

25. Kelvin (K) — Temperature must be expressed in kelvin, not degrees Celsius, in this calculation.

Teacher note: Award one point for each conceptually correct answer. Accept standard biological synonyms and appropriate notation, but use the clue’s defining detail to distinguish closely related structures and mechanisms.
