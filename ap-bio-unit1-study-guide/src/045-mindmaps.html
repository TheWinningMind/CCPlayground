How to Study with a Mind Map

  4. Find the links between maps. Dehydration synthesis, hydrolysis, and hydrogen bonds appear on every map. Why?

Mind Map 1 · Carbohydrates

Chemical Composition

  • Made of C, H, and O; general formula (CH2O)n (1 : 2 : 1)
  • Many hydroxyl (–OH) groups → polar, hydrophilic; form hydrogen bonds with water
  • Also contain a carbonyl (C=O) group
  • Most sugars form rings in water
  • Properties depend on size, structure, and functional groups

Monomers: Monosaccharides

  • Simplest sugars; examples: glucose, fructose, galactose
  • Usually 3–7 carbons: triose, pentose, hexose
  • Aldose: carbonyl at the end (glucose)
  • Ketose: carbonyl in the middle (fructose)
  • Exist in linear and ring forms

Disaccharides

  • Two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic linkage
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose
  • Maltose = glucose + glucose
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose

Building & Breaking

  • Dehydration synthesis: joins monosaccharides, removes H2O, forms a glycosidic linkage
  • Hydrolysis: adds H2O, breaks the linkage, releases smaller sugars
Carbohydrates(CH2O)n
energy · storage · structure

Storage Polysaccharides

  • Starch (plants): amylose, unbranched α(1→4); amylopectin, α(1→4) with α(1→6) branches; stored in seeds and tubers
  • Glycogen (animals): very highly branched; stored in liver and muscle; many ends allow rapid glucose release

Structural Polysaccharides

  • Cellulose (plants): β(1→4) linkages; straight chains hydrogen bond into strong microfibrils in cell walls; humans cannot digest it
  • Chitin: β(1→4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine; fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons

Functions

  • Energy source: glucose fuels cellular respiration
  • Energy storage: starch and glycogen
  • Structural support: cellulose and chitin
  • Cell recognition: carbohydrate chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids

Key AP Connections

  • Structure determines function: α vs. β linkages make starch digestible and cellulose structural
  • Membranes: glycolipids and glycoproteins
  • Enzymes are specific to the linkages they hydrolyze
Same monomer, different linkage, different function.

Mind Map 2 · Lipids

What Are Lipids?

  • Diverse group of mostly nonpolar, hydrophobic molecules
  • Mostly C and H, with little O
  • Not polymers—no repeating monomers
  • Insoluble in water
  • Include fats, phospholipids, steroids, and waxes

Fatty Acids

  • Long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxyl group (–COOH)
  • Saturated: no C=C double bonds; straight chains pack tightly; solid at room temperature (animal fats)
  • Unsaturated: one or more cis C=C double bonds; kinked chains; liquid at room temperature (plant oils)
  • Saturation affects membrane fluidity

Triglycerides (Fats & Oils)

  • Glycerol + 3 fatty acids joined by ester linkages
  • Formed by dehydration synthesis (releases 3 H2O)
  • Nonpolar; main long-term energy store in animals

Waxes

  • Long-chain fatty acid bonded to a long-chain alcohol
  • Highly hydrophobic: waterproofing and protection (plant cuticles, feathers)
Lipidshydrophobic

Phospholipids

  • Glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group (often with a small polar group such as choline)
  • Amphipathic: hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails
  • Form the bilayer of cell membranes: heads face water, tails face inward

Steroids

  • Four fused carbon rings
  • Cholesterol: in animal membranes, helps regulate fluidity; precursor of steroid hormones
  • Steroid hormones: testosterone, estrogen, cortisol

Functions

  • Long-term energy storage (triglycerides)
  • Membrane structure (phospholipids, cholesterol)
  • Signaling (steroid hormones)
  • Insulation and cushioning (fats)
  • Waterproofing (waxes)
  • Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)

Key Takeaways

  • Grouped by being hydrophobic, not by having monomers
  • Amphipathic phospholipids make membranes possible
  • Kinked unsaturated tails keep membranes fluid in the cold
Structure determines function: tail shape controls packing, packing controls fluidity.

Mind Map 3 · Proteins

Amino Acids (Monomers)

  • 20 kinds used in proteins
  • Central carbon bonded to an amino group (–NH2), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen, and an R group
  • Only the R group differs; it sets size, charge, and polarity

Peptide Bonds & Polypeptides

  • Dehydration synthesis between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino group of the next; releases H2O
  • A chain of amino acids is a polypeptide
  • Directionality: N-terminus → C-terminus

Levels of Structure

  • 1° Primary: amino acid sequence; determines all higher levels
  • 2° Secondary: α helix and β pleated sheet; hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H
  • 3° Tertiary: overall 3-D shape of one polypeptide; R-group interactions
  • 4° Quaternary: two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g., hemoglobin)

Denaturation

  • Loss of 3-D shape (2°, 3°, 4°)
  • Caused by heat, extreme pH, high salt, or chemicals
  • Disrupts weak interactions, not peptide bonds
  • Results in loss of function
Proteinspolymers of
amino acids

R-Group Properties

  • Nonpolar (hydrophobic): e.g., Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Phe
  • Polar (hydrophilic): e.g., Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln
  • Charged, acidic (−): Asp, Glu
  • Charged, basic (+): Lys, Arg, His
  • R groups drive folding, solubility, and function

Interactions That Stabilize Shape

  • Hydrogen bonds: polar R groups or backbone
  • Ionic bonds: oppositely charged R groups
  • Hydrophobic interactions: nonpolar R groups cluster inside
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between cysteines
  • Van der Waals interactions: weak, short-range attractions

Functions

  • Enzymes: catalyze reactions
  • Structure: collagen, keratin
  • Transport: hemoglobin, channel proteins
  • Movement: actin, myosin
  • Signaling: peptide hormones, receptors
  • Defense: antibodies

Key Takeaways

  • Sequence → shape → function
  • One amino acid change can alter shape and function (sickle-cell hemoglobin)
  • Shape changes from heat or pH alter enzyme activity
Structure determines function: the amino acid sequence determines the fold.

Mind Map 4 · Nucleic Acids

Nucleotides (Monomers)

  • Phosphate group + pentose sugar + nitrogenous base
  • DNA sugar: deoxyribose; RNA sugar: ribose
  • Bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), thymine (T), uracil (U)
  • Purines: A, G (double ring); pyrimidines: C, T, U (single ring)

DNA Structure

  • Usually double-stranded: a double helix
  • Sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside
  • Bases point inward and pair
  • Strands are antiparallel (5′→3′ and 3′→5′)
  • Nucleotide sequence carries genetic information

RNA Structure

  • Usually single-stranded; can fold by pairing with itself
  • Ribose sugar; bases A, U, G, C
  • Uracil replaces thymine
  • Different RNAs have different roles in gene expression

Building the Polymer

  • Nucleotides join by dehydration synthesis
  • Phosphodiester bonds link the 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next
  • Each strand has a 5′ end and a 3′ end; new nucleotides add to the 3′ end
  • Hydrolysis breaks these bonds by adding water
Nucleic AcidsDNA · RNA

Complementary Base Pairing

  • DNA: A pairs with T; G pairs with C (RNA: A with U)
  • A–T: 2 hydrogen bonds; G–C: 3 hydrogen bonds
  • Each strand specifies the sequence of its partner
  • Allows accurate copying of genetic information

DNA vs. RNA

  • Sugar: deoxyribose vs. ribose
  • Base: thymine (T) vs. uracil (U)
  • Strands: usually double vs. usually single
  • Both: A, G, C, a sugar–phosphate backbone, and 5′/3′ ends

Functions

  • DNA stores and transmits hereditary information
  • Base sequence encodes information
  • RNA helps use that information to make proteins
  • Changes in sequence (mutations) can change the encoded protein

Key AP Connections

  • Sequence matters, not just the number of nucleotides
  • Base pairing is the basis of replication and transcription (Unit 6)
  • G–C-rich DNA needs more energy to separate
Structure determines function: the order of bases stores the information.
