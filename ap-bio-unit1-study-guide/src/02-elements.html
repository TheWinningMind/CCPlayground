Elements of Life

Learning Targets

Living Things Are Built from Recycled Atoms

Organisms do not create matter. Every atom in your body was obtained from your environment—from the food you eat, the water you drink, and the air you breathe. Organisms must constantly take in atoms and small molecules and use them to build new molecules for growth, repair, and reproduction. When organisms die or excrete wastes, decomposers return those atoms to the environment, where they can be used again. This is why matter is said to cycle through ecosystems.

Energy, in contrast, flows in one direction. Organisms capture energy (from sunlight or from chemical bonds), use it to build and maintain complex molecules, and ultimately lose it as heat. Building large, ordered molecules from small ones requires an input of energy; breaking them down can release energy. You will study energy in depth in Unit 3, but the principle starts here: living systems need a constant supply of both matter and energy.

The Six Key Elements: CHNOPS

About 96% of the mass of most organisms comes from just four elements: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Add phosphorus and sulfur and you have the six elements that make up the bulk of biological macromolecules. A handy memory device is CHNOPS.

Must-Know Element Rules
  • Carbohydrates contain C, H, and O, usually in a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio (CH2O)n. No N, P, or S in simple sugars.
  • Lipids are mostly C and H with a little O. Phospholipids also contain P (and often N).
  • Proteins always contain C, H, O, and N. Many contain S.
  • Nucleic acids always contain C, H, O, N, and P.

A common exam setup: “A researcher grows cells with radioactive 35S (or 32P or 15N). Which molecules will become labeled?” Sulfur labels proteins; phosphorus labels nucleic acids (and phospholipids and ATP); nitrogen labels both proteins and nucleic acids.

Why Carbon?

Carbon has four valence electrons, so it can form four covalent bonds. This lets carbon atoms bond to each other and to many other elements, producing chains, branches, and rings of nearly limitless variety. Carbon can also form single, double, and even triple bonds. This versatility is why carbon forms the skeleton of every biological macromolecule.

Attached to these carbon skeletons are functional groups—small clusters of atoms that give molecules their characteristic chemical behavior. You do not need to memorize every functional group for the AP exam, but recognizing the common ones will help you read molecular diagrams.

Misconception Alert

“Plants get their mass from the soil.” They don’t—most of a plant’s dry mass comes from carbon fixed out of CO2 in the air. Soil supplies smaller amounts of essential elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur. Likewise, the carbon atoms in your body came from the food you ate, which originally came from CO2 fixed by producers.

Exam Tip

If a question describes an organism that cannot obtain enough of an element, trace which molecules it cannot build. A plant in nitrogen-poor soil cannot make enough amino acids and nucleotides, so protein synthesis and DNA replication (and therefore growth) slow down. A plant short on phosphorus cannot make enough nucleic acids, ATP, or phospholipids for new membranes.

Introduction to Biological Macromolecules

Learning Targets
  • Distinguish between monomers and polymers.
  • Describe how dehydration synthesis forms covalent bonds between monomers.
  • Describe how hydrolysis breaks covalent bonds between monomers.
  • Explain the roles of water and enzymes in building and breaking polymers.

Monomers and Polymers

Biological macromolecules are large molecules built from smaller, repeating subunits. The subunits are called monomers (“one part”), and the chains they form are called polymers (“many parts”). Three of the four classes of macromolecules are true polymers. Lipids are the exception: they are not built from a chain of repeating monomers, although they are assembled by the same type of reaction.

Dehydration Synthesis: Building Polymers

Monomers are joined by covalent bonds in a reaction called dehydration synthesis (also called a condensation reaction). One monomer gives up a hydroxyl group (–OH) and the other gives up a hydrogen atom (–H). These combine to form a molecule of water, which is released, and the two monomers become covalently bonded.

  • “De-hydration” = removing water. “Synthesis” = building.
  • For each new bond formed, one water molecule is released. Building a chain of n monomers releases n − 1 water molecules.
  • Dehydration synthesis requires energy and is catalyzed by enzymes.

Hydrolysis: Breaking Polymers

Hydrolysis (“water-splitting”) is the reverse of dehydration synthesis. A water molecule is added across the covalent bond between two monomers. The water splits: an –H attaches to one monomer and an –OH attaches to the other, and the bond breaks.

  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes in your digestive system hydrolyze starch into glucose, proteins into amino acids, and fats into glycerol and fatty acids, so the monomers can be absorbed and reused.
  • Hydrolysis of polymers generally releases energy. For example, the hydrolysis of ATP releases energy that cells use to do work.

Quick Math

A polypeptide of 150 amino acids has 149 peptide bonds, and its synthesis released 149 water molecules. Completely hydrolyzing it requires 149 water molecules. Rule: n monomers → n − 1 bonds → n − 1 waters. (For a triglyceride, glycerol + 3 fatty acids forms 3 ester bonds and releases 3 waters.)

The Role of Enzymes

Both dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis are catalyzed by enzymes, proteins that speed up chemical reactions without being consumed. Without enzymes, these reactions would happen far too slowly to sustain life. Each enzyme is specific to a particular type of bond, which is why humans can hydrolyze starch but not cellulose (see Topic 1.5).

Misconception Alert

Mixing up the names. Students often reverse these terms. Anchor them to the word roots: hydro = water, lysis = splitting. Hydrolysis uses water to split. Dehydration means “losing water,” and that water is lost as the bond is made.

Key Terms
Monomer
A small subunit that serves as a building block of a polymer.
Polymer
A long molecule made of many similar or identical monomers linked by covalent bonds.
Macromolecule
A very large molecule, such as a polysaccharide, protein, or nucleic acid.
Dehydration synthesis
A reaction that joins two molecules by removing a water molecule.
Hydrolysis
A reaction that breaks a bond by adding a water molecule.
Enzyme
A biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up reactions.
