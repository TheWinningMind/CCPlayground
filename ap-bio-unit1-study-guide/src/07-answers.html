Answers: Check Your Understanding

Topic 1.1

1. (B) Oxygen’s greater electronegativity creates partial charges, and the bent shape keeps them from canceling. (A) reverses electronegativity and shape. (C) The bonds are polar covalent, not ionic. (D) Hydrogen bonding is a result of polarity, not its cause.
2. (B) Water adheres to the glass and cohesion pulls more water up behind it: capillary action. The other properties do not move water upward.
3. (A) Energy absorbed by water first breaks hydrogen bonds, so water temperature changes slowly and moderates nearby air temperatures.
4. (C) Nonpolar water would not form hydrogen bonds, so far less energy would be needed to evaporate it; evaporation would carry away much less heat. (A) Nonpolar water would dissolve oils better. (B) The boiling point would be lower. (D) is not supported.
5. (C) A long hydrocarbon chain is nonpolar and hydrophobic. Ions (A), polar sugars (B), and charged amino acids (D) all interact with water.
6. (B) In ice, stable hydrogen bonds lock molecules into a spacious lattice that is less dense than liquid water, so ice floats and insulates the water below.

Topic 1.2

1. (B) Sulfur is found in the amino acids cysteine and methionine. DNA contains no sulfur; carbohydrates and triglycerides contain no sulfur.
2. (D) Every amino acid has an amino group, and every nucleotide has a nitrogenous base. Simple carbohydrates and triglycerides contain no nitrogen.
3. (A) Phosphorus is required for nucleotides (DNA) and phospholipids (membranes). Cellulose, starch, and triglycerides are made only of C, H, and O.
4. (C) Carbon’s four valence electrons allow four covalent bonds, enabling diverse skeletons. (A) is false; (B) and (D) describe the wrong bond types.
5. (C) Most plant dry mass is carbon fixed from atmospheric CO2 during photosynthesis. Soil minerals and water contribute far less dry mass.

Topic 1.3

1. (C) An unbranched chain of 40 monomers has 39 glycosidic linkages, and each released one water molecule.
2. (B) Hydrolysis adds water to break a covalent bond. (A) describes dehydration synthesis.
3. (A) Digestion breaks food proteins by hydrolysis; cells then build new proteins by dehydration synthesis.
4. (D) Lipids are not built from repeating monomers.
5. (A) Hydrolysis consumes water, so water decreases as glucose (the monomer) is produced.

Topic 1.4

1. (C) All amino acids share the amino group, carboxyl group, and hydrogen; only the R group differs.
2. (B) Cis double bonds in unsaturated fatty acids create kinks that prevent tight packing, so oils stay liquid. (D) reverses the facts: butter is mostly saturated.
3. (B) The 5′ end has a free phosphate on the 5′ carbon. N- and C-terminus describe polypeptides.
4. (D) The interior of the bilayer is hydrophobic, so nonpolar R groups interact favorably with it. Charged or polar R groups would be excluded.
5. (A) Hydrophilic heads face water while hydrophobic tails cluster inside, away from water. Phospholipids are not polymers (B); four rings describes steroids (C).
6. (C) A peptide bond links the carboxyl carbon of one amino acid to the amino nitrogen of the next, releasing water.

Topic 1.5

1. (B) Backbone hydrogen bonds (C=O ··· H–N) form α helices and β sheets, which make up secondary structure.
2. (C) A large pH change alters the charges on R groups, disrupting the ionic and hydrogen bonds that maintain the shape of the active site. Denaturation does not break peptide bonds (A) or change the sequence (B).
3. (A) A substitution that conserves size and polarity, on the surface, is unlikely to disrupt folding.
4. (D) Human enzymes such as amylase are specific for α-glycosidic linkages and cannot hydrolyze the β linkages in cellulose. Cellulose contains no nitrogen (A), starch is a carbohydrate (B), and cellulose molecules are very large (C).
5. (B) Kinked, unsaturated tails prevent tight packing, keeping membranes fluid at cold temperatures.
6. (D) Quaternary structure is the association of multiple polypeptide subunits.

Topic 1.6

1. (A) G = 35%, so C = 35%; G + C = 70%, leaving 30% for A + T, so T = 15%.
2. (A) RNA uses uracil and ribose, which has a 2′ –OH. Thymine and deoxyribose (B) are DNA features; (C) and (D) are shared by both.
3. (C) Pair each base (G–C, A–T): the complement reads 3′–CTAAGT–5′. Rewritten 5′ to 3′, it is 5′–TGAATC–3′. (A) has the right bases but the wrong orientation; (D) uses uracil, which is not in DNA.
4. (C) G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds versus two for A–T, so more energy is needed to separate G–C-rich DNA.
5. (B) Pairing a double-ring purine with a single-ring pyrimidine keeps every base pair the same width.

6. (C) Phosphorus plus nitrogen plus a pentose sugar identifies a nucleotide. Amino acids lack P; fatty acids and monosaccharides lack N and P. (1.2, 1.4)
7. (B) Cysteine has a sulfhydryl (–SH) group; two cysteines can form an S–S covalent bond. (1.2, 1.5)

Section II: Scoring Guidelines

Question 1 (9 points)

(a) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Describes the difference: saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds (maximum hydrogens); unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds.
  • 1 pt — Explains packing: double bonds create kinks that prevent tight packing, increasing membrane fluidity; straight saturated tails pack tightly, decreasing fluidity.

(b) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Independent variable: growth temperature. Dependent variable: percentage of unsaturated fatty acids in membrane phospholipids. (Both required.)
  • 1 pt — Any one controlled variable: the bacterial species/genetically identical populations, the nutrient medium, growth time (24 hours), number of cultures, extraction method.

(c) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Correct axes and labels: x-axis = growth temperature (°C); y-axis = unsaturated fatty acids (%); appropriate scale; points plotted correctly (bar or line graph acceptable because temperature is continuous; a line or scatter plot is preferred).
  • 1 pt — Error bars plotted correctly (e.g., 68 ± 3 spans 65–71).

(d) 1 point — Describes an inverse (negative) relationship: as growth temperature increases, the percentage of unsaturated fatty acids decreases. Predicts a value of about 51–52% at 25°C (accept 49–54%).

(e) 2 points

  • 1 pt — Explains the benefit: at low temperatures, membranes tend to become rigid; more kinked, unsaturated tails keep the membrane fluid so that membrane proteins can function and substances can move across it.
  • 1 pt — Predicts that the mutant would grow more slowly (or not at all) at 10°C, with justification: with only saturated tails, its membranes would pack tightly and become too rigid at low temperature, impairing transport and other membrane functions.

Question 2 (4 points)

  • (a) 1 pt — Describes any two: hydrophobic interactions among nonpolar R groups; hydrogen bonds between polar R groups; ionic bonds between oppositely charged R groups; covalent disulfide bridges between cysteines.
  • (b) 1 pt — Predicts that enzyme activity will decrease or be lost.
  • (c) 1 pt — Justifies: a charged R group cannot participate in hydrophobic interactions in the nonpolar core and is energetically unfavorable there; the protein misfolds, altering tertiary structure and the shape of the active site, even though the mutation is far from the active site.
  • (d) 1 pt — Explains that isoleucine is also nonpolar and of similar size, so it can still participate in the hydrophobic interactions in the core; the overall fold, and thus the active site, is largely preserved.

Question 3 (4 points)

  • (a) 1 pt — 20% (100 − 30 − 30 − 20 = 20; also, %C = %G in double-stranded DNA).
  • (b) 2 pts — 1 pt: identifies Sample 2. 1 pt: justifies that %A ≠ %T (24 vs. 32) and %G ≠ %C (18 vs. 26), so the bases are not complementary-paired; Chargaff’s rules apply only to double-stranded DNA. (The sample contains thymine, so it is DNA rather than RNA.)
  • (c) 1 pt — Sample 3, because it has a higher G–C content (64% vs. 40%), and G–C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds compared with two for A–T pairs, so more energy is needed to separate the strands.
