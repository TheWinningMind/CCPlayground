TOPIC 1.4

Properties of Biological Macromolecules

Learning Targets
  • Describe the structure of the monomers of carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids, and the components of lipids.
  • Explain how the R group of an amino acid determines its chemical properties (nonpolar, polar, or charged).
  • Explain the directionality of nucleic acids (5′ and 3′ ends) and of polypeptides (N-terminus and C-terminus).
  • Distinguish saturated from unsaturated fatty acids and describe the structure of phospholipids.

In this topic you will learn the parts list for each macromolecule. In Topic 1.5 you will see how those parts determine what each molecule does. Focus on being able to recognize and draw each monomer and to identify which parts are polar and which are nonpolar.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates include sugars and the polymers made from them. They are used for energy (glucose, starch, glycogen) and for structure (cellulose, chitin). They are made of C, H, and O.

Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides (simple sugars) are the monomers of carbohydrates. Most have the formula (CH2O)n, with several hydroxyl (–OH) groups that make them polar and water-soluble. Glucose, fructose, and galactose all have the formula C6H12O6 but differ in the arrangement of their atoms, so they are isomers with different properties. Ribose and deoxyribose are five-carbon sugars found in nucleic acids. In water, most five- and six-carbon sugars form rings, and the carbons are numbered 1′, 2′, 3′, and so on.

Disaccharides and Polysaccharides

Two monosaccharides joined by dehydration synthesis form a disaccharide; the covalent bond is a glycosidic linkage. Examples: sucrose (glucose + fructose, table sugar), lactose (glucose + galactose, milk sugar), and maltose (glucose + glucose). Long chains of monosaccharides are polysaccharides.

Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group defined by one shared property: they are mostly nonpolar and hydrophobic, because they consist mostly of hydrocarbon regions (C–H bonds share electrons nearly equally). Lipids are not polymers. The three most important groups are fats, phospholipids, and steroids.

Fats (Triglycerides)

A triglyceride is made of one glycerol molecule (a three-carbon alcohol) joined to three fatty acids by ester linkages formed in dehydration synthesis. Each fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end. Fats store about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrates, and they also provide insulation and cushioning.

  • Saturated fatty acids have no carbon–carbon double bonds; every carbon holds as many hydrogens as possible (“saturated with hydrogen”). The chains are straight and pack tightly, so saturated fats (most animal fats) are solid at room temperature.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more double bonds. A cis double bond causes a kink in the chain that prevents tight packing, so unsaturated fats (most plant and fish oils) are liquid at room temperature.

Phospholipids

A phospholipid has glycerol attached to two fatty acids and a phosphate group (often linked to another small charged or polar group). This gives the molecule a dual personality: it is amphipathic, meaning it has both a hydrophilic region and a hydrophobic region.

  • The phosphate “head” is charged/polar and hydrophilic.
  • The two fatty acid “tails” are nonpolar and hydrophobic.

In water, phospholipids spontaneously arrange into a bilayer: heads face the watery environment on both sides, and tails face inward, shielded from water. This bilayer is the basic structure of every cell membrane (Unit 2).

Steroids

Steroids are lipids built from four fused carbon rings. Cholesterol is a component of animal cell membranes, where it helps regulate fluidity, and it is the starting material for steroid hormones such as estrogen and testosterone. Because steroids are nonpolar, steroid hormones can diffuse directly through the cell membrane.

Proteins

Proteins are the most functionally diverse macromolecules. They act as enzymes, structural supports, transport channels, receptors, hormones, antibodies, and motor proteins. Proteins are polymers of amino acids.

Amino Acid Structure

Every amino acid has a central carbon (the α carbon) bonded to four things:

  1. an amino group (–NH2),
  2. a carboxyl group (–COOH),
  3. a hydrogen atom, and
  4. a variable side chain called the R group.

There are 20 amino acids commonly found in proteins. The first three parts are identical in all of them; only the R group differs. The R group gives each amino acid its unique chemical properties.

Peptide Bonds and Directionality

Amino acids are joined by dehydration synthesis between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next. The resulting covalent bond is a peptide bond. A chain of amino acids is a polypeptide. Every polypeptide has directionality: one end has a free amino group (the N-terminus) and the other has a free carboxyl group (the C-terminus). Amino acids are always added to the C-terminus end, and the sequence is always written from N-terminus to C-terminus.

Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids—DNA and RNA—store, transmit, and help express hereditary information. They are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts:

  1. a five-carbon (pentose) sugar: deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA;
  2. a phosphate group attached to the 5′ carbon of the sugar; and
  3. a nitrogenous base attached to the 1′ carbon of the sugar.

Directionality: Nucleotides link when the phosphate on the 5′ carbon of one nucleotide forms a covalent bond with the hydroxyl on the 3′ carbon of the previous nucleotide. This phosphodiester bond creates a repeating sugar–phosphate backbone, with the bases sticking out to the side. Every strand therefore has a 5′ end (free phosphate) and a 3′ end (free hydroxyl). New nucleotides can be added only to the 3′ end, so nucleic acids are always built in the 5′ → 3′ direction. Topic 1.6 covers DNA and RNA structure in detail.

Misconception Alert
  • “All lipids are polymers.” They are not; lipids are grouped by being hydrophobic, not by having repeating monomers.
  • “Amino acids differ in their amino or carboxyl groups.” Only the R group differs.
  • “Unsaturated fats have no hydrogens.” They have fewer hydrogens than the maximum, because of C=C double bonds.
Exam Tip

When shown a molecular diagram, identify it by its tell-tale features: a ring with many –OH groups is a sugar; long C–H chains are a lipid; N–C–C=O repeating along a chain is a polypeptide; a phosphate–sugar–base unit is a nucleotide. Nitrogen with no phosphorus suggests a protein; nitrogen with phosphorus suggests a nucleic acid.

Key Terms
Monosaccharide
A simple sugar; the monomer of carbohydrates.
Glycosidic linkage
Covalent bond between two monosaccharides.
Isomers
Molecules with the same formula but different structures.
Triglyceride
Glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester linkages.
Saturated fatty acid
No C=C double bonds; straight chain.
Unsaturated fatty acid
One or more C=C double bonds; kinked chain.
Amphipathic
Having both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.
Steroid
A lipid with four fused carbon rings, e.g., cholesterol.
R group
The variable side chain of an amino acid.
Peptide bond
Covalent bond between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next.
N-terminus / C-terminus
The amino and carboxyl ends of a polypeptide.
Nucleotide
Pentose sugar + phosphate + nitrogenous base.
Phosphodiester bond
Links the 3′ carbon of one nucleotide to the 5′ phosphate of the next.
