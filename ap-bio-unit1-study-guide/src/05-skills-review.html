Science Practices and Free-Response Strategy

The AP Biology Exam has two sections: multiple choice and free response. In recent years the free-response section has included two long questions (one of which involves interpreting an experiment) and four short questions that target specific skills such as experimental design, data analysis, models, and evolutionary trees. Always confirm the current format in the official College Board course description. Whatever the format, the scoring rewards the same habits.

Know Your Task Verbs

Every free-response prompt begins with a task verb. Each verb asks for something different, and answering the wrong verb is the fastest way to lose points.

VerbWhat it asks forUnit 1 example of a full-credit response
IdentifyName or state the answer. No explanation needed.“Identify the bond that forms between amino acids.” → A peptide bond.
DescribeGive the relevant characteristics of something.“Describe the structure of a phospholipid.” → A glycerol bonded to two hydrophobic fatty acid tails and a hydrophilic phosphate-containing head.
ExplainGive the how or why; connect cause and effect.“Explain why ice floats.” → Hydrogen bonds lock frozen water molecules into a lattice that holds them farther apart than in liquid water, so ice is less dense.
PredictState an expected outcome. A reason is not required unless asked.“Predict the effect of replacing a hydrophobic amino acid in the core with a charged one.” → The protein will misfold and lose function.
JustifyGive evidence or reasoning that supports a claim or prediction.“Justify your prediction.” → A charged R group would be repelled by the hydrophobic core, disrupting hydrophobic interactions that stabilize tertiary structure.
CalculatePerform a mathematical calculation and show work.“Calculate the percentage of cytosine if adenine is 22%.” → (100 − 44) ÷ 2 = 28%.
Construct / RepresentDraw a graph, table, or diagram.Plot data with a labeled x-axis (independent variable), labeled y-axis (dependent variable), units, a title, and a key if needed.
Support / RefuteUse evidence to argue for or against a claim.Cite specific values from the data, then explain how they support or contradict the claim.

Experimental Design Vocabulary

Independent variable
The factor the experimenter deliberately changes (x-axis).
Dependent variable
The factor that is measured in response (y-axis).
Controlled variables (constants)
Conditions kept the same in all groups so they cannot explain differences in results.
Negative control
A group in which no response is expected; shows what happens without the treatment.
Positive control
A group in which a known response is expected; shows the method can detect an effect.
Null hypothesis
The prediction that the independent variable has no effect.
Error bars
Often ±2 SEx̄ (about 95% confidence). If error bars of two groups do not overlap, the difference is likely significant; if they overlap a lot, it is likely not.

Five Rules for Free-Response Success

  1. Answer in order and label your parts. Readers score each part separately. Write (a), (b), (c) and keep each answer next to its label.
  2. Be specific. Say “hydrogen bonds between the R groups of polar amino acids,” not “bonds in the protein.”
  3. Use “because.” For every explain or justify prompt, make sure your sentence contains a cause and an effect.
  4. Use the data. When a graph or table is given, quote actual numbers with units.
  5. Don’t contradict yourself. Readers do not award credit for a correct answer buried among incorrect ones. Give one clear answer.
The Claim–Evidence–Reasoning Template

Claim: State your answer in one sentence. Evidence: Point to the specific data or structural feature. Reasoning: Explain the biological principle that links the evidence to the claim. Example: “Claim: Enzyme activity decreases above 45°C. Evidence: Activity falls from 9.1 to 1.2 units between 45°C and 60°C. Reasoning: High temperatures break hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that maintain the enzyme’s tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer binds.”

Unit 1 One-Page Review Sheet

1.1 Water

  • O is more electronegative than H → polar covalent bonds; bent shape → polar molecule.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between water molecules (up to 4 each).
  • Cohesion (water–water) → surface tension; transpiration pull.
  • Adhesion (water–other) → capillary action in xylem.
  • High specific heat → stable temperatures (energy breaks H-bonds first).
  • High heat of vaporization → evaporative cooling (sweat, transpiration).
  • Ice less dense (H-bond lattice) → floats, insulates lakes.
  • Solvent for polar and ionic substances (hydration shells); hydrophobic substances excluded.

1.2 Elements

  • CHNOPS. N → proteins + nucleic acids. P → nucleic acids + phospholipids + ATP. S → proteins (cysteine, methionine).
  • Carbon: 4 covalent bonds → chains, rings, branches.
  • Matter cycles; energy flows (lost as heat).

1.3 Building and Breaking

  • Dehydration synthesis: –OH + –H removed as H2O → covalent bond. Builds.
  • Hydrolysis: add H2O → break bond. Breaks (digestion).
  • n monomers → n − 1 bonds → n − 1 waters.
  • Both catalyzed by enzymes.

1.4 Building Blocks

  • Carbs: monosaccharides, glycosidic linkages. Starch/glycogen = storage; cellulose/chitin = structure.
  • Lipids: hydrophobic, not polymers. Triglyceride = glycerol + 3 fatty acids. Saturated = no C=C, straight, solid. Unsaturated = C=C kinks, liquid. Phospholipid = amphipathic → bilayer. Steroid = 4 rings.
  • Proteins: amino acid = amino group + carboxyl + H + R group. R groups nonpolar, polar, or charged. Peptide bonds. N- to C-terminus.
  • Nucleic acids: nucleotide = 5-C sugar + phosphate + base. Phosphodiester bonds; sugar–phosphate backbone. Built 5′ → 3′.

1.5 Structure → Function

  • 1° sequence (peptide bonds) → 2° α/β (backbone H-bonds) → 3° R-group interactions (hydrophobic, H, ionic, disulfide) → 4° multiple chains.
  • Amino acid change → R-group interactions → shape → function (sickle cell: Glu → Val).
  • Denaturation: heat, pH, salt disrupt weak bonds; peptide bonds intact.
  • α vs. β linkage → starch vs. cellulose.
  • Unsaturated tails → more fluid membranes.

1.6 Nucleic Acids

  • DNA: deoxyribose, A/T/G/C, double-stranded. RNA: ribose (2′ –OH), A/U/G/C, single-stranded.
  • Purines A, G (2 rings); pyrimidines C, T, U (1 ring).
  • A=T (2 H-bonds); G≡C (3 H-bonds). Antiparallel strands.
  • Chargaff: %A = %T, %G = %C (dsDNA).
