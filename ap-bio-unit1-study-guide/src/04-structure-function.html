TOPIC 1.5

Structure and Function of Biological Macromolecules

Learning Targets
  • Explain how the sequence and chemical properties of monomers determine the structure and function of a polymer.
  • Describe the four levels of protein structure and the bonds or interactions that stabilize each.
  • Predict how a change in a monomer’s sequence or in environmental conditions affects a polymer’s structure and function.
  • Explain how differences in glycosidic linkages and in fatty acid saturation lead to different functions.

The Central Principle

The function of a macromolecule depends on its three-dimensional shape, and its shape depends on the sequence and chemical properties of its monomers. Change the monomers, or the order of the monomers, and you may change the shape—and therefore the function. This idea runs through all four classes of macromolecules, but it is most dramatic in proteins.

The Four Levels of Protein Structure

LevelWhat it isWhat holds it together
Primary (1°)The unique sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide, determined by the gene that codes for itCovalent peptide bonds
Secondary (2°)Local, repeating folds: the α helix (coil) and the β pleated sheetHydrogen bonds between atoms of the polypeptide backbone (not the R groups)
Tertiary (3°)The overall three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptideInteractions between R groups: hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar R groups cluster inside), hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and covalent disulfide bridges between cysteines
Quaternary (4°)Association of two or more polypeptide subunits into one functional protein. Only some proteins have it.The same interactions as tertiary structure, between subunits
Misconception Alert

Secondary vs. tertiary bonds. Both involve hydrogen bonds, but in secondary structure they form between backbone atoms (the C=O of one amino acid and the N–H of another), whereas in tertiary structure they form between R groups. Also, quaternary structure is not “just a more folded” protein—it requires more than one polypeptide chain.

When Protein Structure Changes

Changes in Sequence

Because the primary structure determines how the protein folds, even a single amino acid substitution can alter the protein’s shape and function. The classic example is sickle-cell disease. In the β-globin chains of hemoglobin, a single amino acid—glutamic acid, which has a charged, hydrophilic R group—is replaced with valine, which has a nonpolar, hydrophobic R group. The new hydrophobic patch on the surface causes hemoglobin molecules to stick together into long fibers when oxygen levels are low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape that can block small blood vessels.

Not every change is harmful. A substitution with an amino acid of similar properties (for example, one nonpolar R group for another), or a change in a region not critical to the protein’s shape, may have little or no effect. When predicting the effect of a substitution, ask: Does the new R group have different chemical properties (size, polarity, charge)? Is the change in a functionally important region, such as an enzyme’s active site?

Changes in Environment: Denaturation

A protein’s shape is held together largely by weak interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions). Conditions that disrupt these interactions can cause a protein to unfold, or denature, and lose its function:

  • High temperature increases molecular motion enough to break hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions.
  • Changes in pH alter the charges on acidic and basic R groups, disrupting ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds.
  • High salt concentrations or certain chemicals interfere with ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions.

Denaturation does not usually break peptide bonds, so the primary structure remains intact. Some proteins can refold if normal conditions return, but many cannot.

Exam Tip

A strong “predict and justify” answer about a protein follows this chain: change in amino acid (or condition) → change in the interactions between R groups → change in 3-D shape → change in function (for example, the substrate no longer fits the active site). Name the specific interaction that is disrupted whenever you can.

Carbohydrates: The Linkage Determines Function

Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose, yet one is food and the other is wood. The difference lies in the type of glucose and the resulting glycosidic linkage.

  • Starch and glycogen use α-glucose with α(1→4) linkages (plus α(1→6) branch points). The chains coil into helices and are easily hydrolyzed by enzymes such as amylase, making them good energy stores. Glycogen’s extensive branching gives it many ends from which glucose can be released quickly.
  • Cellulose uses β-glucose with β(1→4) linkages, in which every other glucose is flipped. The chains are straight, and the –OH groups of parallel chains form many hydrogen bonds with one another, producing strong, rigid microfibrils—ideal for structural support in plant cell walls.

Most animals, including humans, lack enzymes that can hydrolyze β(1→4) linkages, so cellulose passes through our digestive tract as fiber. Cows and termites can digest cellulose only because of microorganisms in their guts that produce the necessary enzymes.

Lipids: Saturation Determines Fluidity

The structure of fatty acid tails controls how tightly lipids pack. In membranes, phospholipids with unsaturated (kinked) tails cannot pack closely, which keeps the membrane more fluid, especially at low temperatures. Phospholipids with saturated (straight) tails pack tightly, making the membrane less fluid. Organisms adjust membrane composition to their environment: cold-adapted organisms tend to have a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids in their membranes.

Nucleic Acids: Sequence Stores Information

The function of DNA and RNA lies in the sequence of their nitrogenous bases. This sequence encodes the information used to build proteins. A change in the base sequence (a mutation) can change the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein, which can change the protein’s shape and function. Thus, structure determines function at two levels: the sequence of DNA determines the sequence of a protein, which determines its shape and activity.

Nucleic Acids

Learning Targets

DNA and RNA Compared

FeatureDNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)RNA (ribonucleic acid)
SugarDeoxyribose — has an H at the 2′ carbon (“de-oxy” = missing an oxygen)Ribose — has an –OH at the 2′ carbon
BasesAdenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), thymine (T)Adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), uracil (U)
StrandsUsually double-stranded (double helix)Usually single-stranded; can fold into complex shapes by base pairing within itself
FunctionLong-term storage of genetic informationCarries and helps express information (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA); some RNAs act as catalysts
StabilityMore chemically stableLess stable; the 2′ –OH makes it more reactive

Shared features: Both are polymers of nucleotides; both have a sugar–phosphate backbone joined by phosphodiester bonds; both have 5′ and 3′ ends; both use A, G, and C.

Nitrogenous Bases: Purines and Pyrimidines

  • Purines have a double-ring structure: adenine (A) and guanine (G). Memory hook: “Pure As Gold.”
  • Pyrimidines have a single-ring structure: cytosine (C), thymine (T), and uracil (U). Memory hook: “CUT the Py.”

In the double helix, a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine. A double-ring base paired with a single-ring base gives every rung of the DNA “ladder” the same width, so the helix has a uniform diameter.

The Structure of the Double Helix

  • Sugar–phosphate backbones run along the outside of the helix, held together by covalent phosphodiester bonds. The negatively charged phosphates face the watery environment.
  • Nitrogenous bases point inward and pair across the two strands by hydrogen bonds.
  • Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G pairs with C (three hydrogen bonds). In RNA, A pairs with U.
  • The two strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions. One strand runs 5′ → 3′ from top to bottom while its partner runs 3′ → 5′.
Why These Structural Facts Matter
  • Complementary pairing means each strand can serve as a template for building the other, which is the basis of DNA replication and transcription (Unit 6).
  • Hydrogen bonds between bases are individually weak, so the strands can be separated (“unzipped”) for copying, yet millions of them together keep the molecule stable.
  • G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds, so DNA with a higher G–C content requires more energy (a higher temperature) to separate its strands.
  • 5′ → 3′ synthesis: nucleotides can only be added to the free 3′ –OH of a growing strand. This is why the two strands are copied differently during replication.
Chargaff’s Rules — Quick Math

In double-stranded DNA, the amount of A equals T, and the amount of G equals C. So if a DNA sample is 20% adenine, then T = 20%, A + T = 40%, leaving 60% for G + C, so G = 30% and C = 30%. (These rules apply to double-stranded DNA, not to single-stranded RNA.)

Writing a complementary strand: For 5′–ATGCCA–3′, the complementary DNA strand is 3′–TACGGT–5′, which, written 5′ to 3′, is 5′–TGGCAT–3′. Always label the ends.

Misconception Alert
  • “The two strands are held together by covalent bonds.” No—the strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between bases. Covalent bonds hold each strand’s backbone together.
  • “Antiparallel means the strands are twisted.” Antiparallel refers to direction (5′→3′ vs. 3′→5′), not to the helix shape.
  • Forgetting uracil. RNA uses U instead of T. If a question asks for the RNA complement of A, the answer is U.
