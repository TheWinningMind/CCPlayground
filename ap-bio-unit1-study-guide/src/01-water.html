TOPIC 1.1

Structure of Water and Hydrogen Bonding

Learning Targets
  • Explain how the structure of a water molecule makes it polar.
  • Describe how hydrogen bonds form between water molecules and between water and other polar substances.
  • Explain how hydrogen bonding produces cohesion, adhesion, surface tension, high specific heat, high heat of vaporization, and the lower density of ice.
  • Explain why water is an excellent solvent for polar and ionic substances but not for nonpolar ones.

Water Is a Polar Molecule

A water molecule (H2O) consists of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms. These are polar covalent bonds: the electrons are shared, but not equally. Oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen, meaning it pulls shared electrons more strongly toward itself.

As a result, the oxygen end of the molecule carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and each hydrogen carries a partial positive charge (δ+). Because the molecule is bent (about 104.5° between the two O–H bonds) rather than straight, these charges do not cancel. The whole molecule has a negative side and a positive side. A molecule with an uneven distribution of charge like this is called polar.

Hydrogen Bonds

A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between a partially positive hydrogen atom (already covalently bonded to an electronegative atom such as oxygen or nitrogen) and a partially negative atom on another molecule, or on a different part of the same molecule. In liquid water, hydrogen bonds constantly form, break, and re-form.

Any single hydrogen bond is weak—much weaker than a covalent bond. But because there are enormous numbers of them, together they give water unusual and biologically essential properties. Hydrogen bonds also hold together the two strands of DNA and help stabilize the folded shapes of proteins, so you will meet them again in Topics 1.5 and 1.6.

Misconception Alert

Hydrogen bonds are between water molecules, not inside them. The bonds holding O and H together within one water molecule are polar covalent bonds. A hydrogen bond is the attraction from one molecule to the next. On the exam, labeling the O–H bond inside a molecule as a “hydrogen bond” is a classic wrong answer.

Emergent Properties of Water

Each property below is a direct consequence of polarity and hydrogen bonding. For every one, learn the property, the reason, and an example in a living system.

1. Cohesion and Surface Tension

Cohesion is the attraction of water molecules to each other through hydrogen bonds. At the surface of a body of water, molecules are hydrogen bonded to neighbors beside and below them but not above, so they pull together tightly and create surface tension—a surface that resists being broken.

Biological examples: Cohesion helps hold a continuous column of water together as it is pulled up through the xylem of a tall tree during transpiration. Surface tension allows small organisms such as water striders to walk on a pond surface.

2. Adhesion and Capillary Action

Adhesion is the attraction of water molecules to other polar or charged surfaces. When water adheres to the walls of a narrow tube while cohesion pulls more water along behind it, the water climbs upward against gravity. This is capillary action.

Biological examples: Water adheres to the cellulose walls of xylem vessels, which helps counteract gravity as water moves up a plant. Adhesion and cohesion working together are why water can move from roots to leaves.

3. High Specific Heat

Specific heat is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1°C. Water’s is very high (about 4.18 J/g·°C). When water is heated, much of the energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds before the molecules can move faster, so the temperature rises slowly. When water cools, hydrogen bonds form and release heat, so the temperature falls slowly.

Biological examples: Large bodies of water stay at a fairly stable temperature, giving aquatic organisms a stable environment and moderating coastal climates. Because organisms are mostly water, they resist sudden changes in internal temperature.

4. High Heat of Vaporization

For a water molecule to evaporate, its hydrogen bonds to neighbors must be broken, which takes a lot of energy. Water therefore has a high heat of vaporization. When water evaporates from a surface, the molecules that leave carry a large amount of heat energy away with them. This is evaporative cooling.

Biological examples: Sweating in mammals and transpiration from leaves both cool the organism as water evaporates.

5. Ice Is Less Dense Than Liquid Water

Most substances are densest as solids. Water is unusual. As water freezes, each molecule becomes locked into a stable crystal lattice in which it is hydrogen bonded to four neighbors. This lattice holds the molecules farther apart than in liquid water, so ice is less dense and floats.

Biological example: A layer of floating ice insulates the liquid water below it, so lakes and ponds freeze from the top down rather than freezing solid. Aquatic organisms can survive the winter in the liquid water beneath the ice.

6. Water Is an Excellent Solvent

Water is often called the “universal solvent,” although it does not dissolve everything. Water dissolves polar and ionic substances well. When a salt such as NaCl enters water, the δ− oxygens surround each Na+ ion and the δ+ hydrogens surround each Cl− ion, forming hydration shells that pull the ions apart and keep them in solution. Polar molecules such as glucose dissolve because they can form hydrogen bonds with water.

  • Hydrophilic (“water-loving”) substances are polar or charged and interact with water. Examples: sugars, ions, many amino acids.
  • Hydrophobic (“water-fearing”) substances are nonpolar and do not interact favorably with water. Examples: oils, fats, the fatty acid tails of phospholipids.

Hydrophobic molecules cluster together in water—not because they strongly attract each other, but because water molecules hydrogen bond to one another and exclude them. These hydrophobic interactions drive the formation of cell membranes and the folding of proteins.

Exam Tip

A complete explanation of any water property has three links: (1) water is polar → (2) so it forms hydrogen bonds → (3) which causes the property. For example: “Water has a high specific heat because energy must be used to break hydrogen bonds between polar water molecules before their kinetic energy, and therefore temperature, can increase.” Answers that state the property without the hydrogen-bond link usually do not earn the explanation point.

Acids, Bases, and pH (Background)

A small fraction of water molecules dissociate into hydrogen ions (H+) and hydroxide ions (OH−). The pH scale (0–14) measures the concentration of H+: pH = −log[H+]. Acids increase [H+] and lower the pH; bases decrease [H+] and raise the pH. Each one-unit change in pH is a tenfold change in [H+], so a solution at pH 4 has 100 times more H+ than one at pH 6. Buffers resist changes in pH and help keep conditions stable in cells and blood (pH about 7.4). pH matters in Unit 1 because changes in pH can disrupt the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold proteins in their functional shape.

Key Terms
Polar covalent bond
A covalent bond in which electrons are shared unequally.
Electronegativity
An atom’s attraction for shared electrons.
Hydrogen bond
A weak attraction between a δ+ hydrogen and a δ− atom (often O or N) of another molecule or region.
Cohesion
Attraction between molecules of the same substance.
Adhesion
Attraction between molecules of different substances.
Surface tension
Resistance of a liquid’s surface to being stretched or broken.
Capillary action
Movement of water up a narrow space due to adhesion and cohesion.
Specific heat
Energy needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1°C.
Heat of vaporization
Energy needed to convert a liquid to a gas.
Hydrophilic
Polar or charged; dissolves in or interacts with water.
Hydrophobic
Nonpolar; does not interact favorably with water.
Solvent / solute
The dissolving agent / the substance dissolved.
